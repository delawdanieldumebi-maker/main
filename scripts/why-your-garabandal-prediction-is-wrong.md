## 01:26 — [MISTAKE 1 — TREATING RUMORS AS CLUES]

The first mistake is the easiest one to make. It's treating everything you read as if Conchita said it.

She didn't.

Over sixty-five years, Garabandal has collected a cloud of details. Some came from people who sat across a table from the seers. Others came from a forum post or a video that quoted another video. On your screen, they all look the same.

Here is what has a name attached to it. In 1965, a Mexican priest, Father Gustavo Morelos, spent time in the village. Conchita told him the miracle would not happen in May. Later, an author named Harry Daley recorded her saying it would come in April or May. Put those two side by side and you're left with April.

In 1988, an American named Glenn Hudson called Conchita and read her both sources. He asked if April was right. She didn't confirm it. She didn't deny it either. She told him, "Do what you want."

Then the other details. The miracle falls on a Thursday, at eight-thirty in the evening, Spanish time. It falls between the eighth and the sixteenth of the month. It falls on the feast of a martyr of the Eucharist. Father Morelos added that this martyr was not Spanish, and that his name was strange to her, one she had never heard. And a woman named María Herrero said Conchita told her it would not fall on a great feast of Our Lord or Our Lady. Not Holy Thursday. An ordinary Thursday.

That's the list. Six working clues. April. A Thursday. The eighth through the sixteenth. A martyr of the Eucharist. Not Spanish. Not a great feast.

Now, here's what is not on the list. You'll read that Mari Loli, another of the four girls, said the Warning would come in an even-numbered year. We went looking for that in her own words. We couldn't find it. It's a rumor, and a rumor can't knock out a date.

That's mistake number one. Every prediction built on rumors is built on sand. Everything from here on uses only the six.

## 03:45 — [WHO THE GIRLS WERE]

If you're new to this story, here is why any of it matters.

San Sebastián de Garabandal sits at the end of a narrow road in the mountains of Cantabria. In 1961, it was a handful of stone houses, cows, and mud. On the evening of June 18th of that year, four girls, Conchita, Mari Loli, Jacinta, and Mari Cruz, slipped off to take apples that weren't theirs. They said an angel appeared to them.

Days later, they said the Virgin Mary came. And she kept coming, for four years.

Witnesses watched them fall into ecstasy, faces turned to the sky. Doctors pricked them with needles. The girls didn't react. Then the ecstasy ended, and they were village kids again, laughing and running down the lanes.

And through those years, they spoke of three events still to come.

First, the Warning. A moment when every person on earth, wherever they are, will see their own soul the way God sees it. Conchita called it a correction of the conscience of the world.

Then the Miracle, at the pines above the village. The sick who are present will be healed. It will last about a quarter of an hour, and it will leave behind a sign that can be photographed but never touched.

And third, a chastisement. Conditional. It only comes if the world refuses the first two.

Conchita knows the date of the second. That's what everyone is trying to crack.

## 05:21 — [MISTAKE 2 — NOT CHECKING THE CALENDAR]

The second mistake is falling in love with a date before you check it.

And that's exactly what happened with 2028.

On paper, 2028 looks beautiful. April 13th that year is a Thursday. It sits right inside the eighth-to-sixteenth window. And it's the feast of Saint Hermenegild. He was a prince in the sixth century who refused to take Easter communion from a bishop he believed was a heretic. His own father, the king, had him put to death for it. A prince who died rather than receive the Eucharist the wrong way.

So people stopped looking.

But open a calendar and look at Easter in 2028. Easter Sunday falls on April 16th. Count back three days. April 13th, 2028 is Holy Thursday.

The one day Conchita specifically said it would not be.

And there's a second problem. Hermenegild was a Visigoth prince who lived and died in what is now Spain. Father Morelos remembered that the martyr would not be Spanish.

So 2028 doesn't fail one clue. It fails two. On the six clues we can actually trace, the favorite date on the internet is out.

Some say the Eucharistic connection is too strong to ignore. But if we start throwing out the clues we don't like, we're not testing anymore. We're just hoping.

## 06:52 — [MISTAKE 3 — FORGETTING THE WARNING COMES FIRST]

The third mistake is the one almost nobody talks about, and it may be the most useful test of all.

People treat the Warning and the Miracle like two separate events on two separate timelines. They aren't.

Mari Loli was the only one of the four who said she knew the year of the Warning. She moved to the United States, raised a family in New England, and died in 2009 without revealing it. But she did say two things. First, that the Warning and the Miracle fall in the same year. And second, she once told a priest she couldn't remember whether the Warning would come three weeks or three months before the Miracle.

Think about what that means.

If the Miracle is in April, the Warning has to come somewhere between January and late March of that same year. Before the miracle, not after it.

Now go back to April 2026. By the time April arrived this year, had every person on earth seen the state of their own soul? No one on earth missed it, because no one can miss it. That's the whole point of the Warning.

So April 2026 was never possible. It wasn't knocked out by the calendar. It was knocked out by the order of events.

And here's what that gives you, starting today. Every year from now on, you have a simple check. If January, February, and March pass with no Warning, that year's April is gone. You don't need a video to tell you. You'll know.

And by the way, 2026 had a second problem. One of its two Thursdays in the window, April 9th, fell inside the octave of Easter, the eight days the Church celebrates as one great feast of the Lord. Not an ordinary Thursday at all.

## 09:02 — [MISTAKE 4 — IGNORING THE SIGNS]

The fourth mistake is jumping straight to the Miracle and skipping over everything the seers said would happen first.

The girls didn't only speak about events. They spoke about signs. And two of them are worth looking at closely, because they're the kind of thing you can actually watch for in the news.

The first is described as an important synod in the Church. Many today believe it points to something recent. The Synod on Synodality began under Pope Francis. When Leo the Fourteenth was elected in May of 2025, he kept it going. And the process is meant to close with a great Church assembly in Rome, planned for October of 2028.

Look at that date carefully. If that assembly is the synod the seers meant, then it hasn't finished yet. And a sign that hasn't finished can't sit behind a miracle in April of that same year.

The second sign is even clearer. It has been reported that the Pope will travel to Moscow, and that after he returns to Rome, hostilities will break out.

No pope has ever made that trip. Not one. John Paul the Second wanted to and never went. Pope Francis spoke about it more than once. There were real conversations, and it still never happened. As of today, Pope Leo has no trip to Russia on his schedule.

So if your prediction lands before a pope ever sets foot in Moscow, you've got a problem. The sign isn't on the board yet.

Now, a fair word. These signs come to us secondhand, through people who knew the seers. We should hold them a little more loosely than the six clues. But a careful person doesn't ignore them either. They're the closest thing we have to a clock on the wall.

## 11:01 — [THE OBJECTION — JOEY LOMANGINO]

At this point, a skeptic would raise his hand. And he'd be right to. Because the strongest argument against Garabandal didn't happen in Spain. It happened here in America.

Joey Lomangino was a New Yorker who lost his sight in an accident when he was sixteen. He went on to spend his life telling Americans about Garabandal.

On March 19th, 1964, Conchita wrote to Joey. She said the Virgin had told her that on the day of the miracle, Joey would receive new eyes.

Joey waited. He waited fifty years. He died on June 18th, 2014, still blind, before any miracle.

The skeptic's case is simple. The letter is real. It was written down in 1964, so nobody can say it was invented later. And it didn't happen.

Those who believe answer in two ways. First, the letter didn't say Joey would see again on earth. It said new eyes. Many now read that as the eyes of heaven, from which he would see the miracle. Second, the date of his death. June 18th is the anniversary of the very first apparition of the angel in 1961. It is also the date of the last public message in 1965. Joey died on that exact anniversary.

Here, we have to be careful. The new-eyes reading came after Joey died, from people who already believed. It can't be proven, and a skeptic will call it moving the goalposts. That's a fair charge. What no one disputes is the date on his death certificate. What you make of it is between you and God.

The lesson for predictions? If even a promise written in Conchita's own hand can be read two ways, how sure should any of us be about a date we built from a YouTube comment?

## 12:59 — [MISTAKE 5 — FORGETTING THE EIGHT-DAY RULE]

The fifth mistake is the biggest, and it's the one that should end every argument.

Conchita has never said the date can be worked out. She said she would announce it. Eight days ahead.

Think about why that matters. Every clue we've tested was let slip in conversations over decades. None was given so we could crack the code. The date belongs to her secret, and the secret belongs to God.

So when someone online tells you they've solved it, they haven't. At best, they've narrowed it down. At worst, they're selling you something.

And this mistake has a cousin. In 1963, Conchita reportedly said that after Pope John the Twenty-Third, there would be only three more popes, and then would come what she called the end of times. People counted. Paul the Sixth. John Paul the First. John Paul the Second. Then they panicked, because the end of the world never came.

But listen closely to how she reportedly explained it. The end of times, she said, is not the end of the world. It's the end of an era. That reading is still argued over, and you'll find people who count the popes differently. But what it shows is how quickly a careful word becomes a headline when we're in a hurry.

So here is the most honest thing anyone can tell you about the date of the miracle. No one on this channel knows it. No one on any channel knows it. One woman in New York knows it. And she'll tell us eight days ahead.

## 14:49 — [WHAT'S LEFT STANDING]

So after five mistakes, what's left?

Let's run the six clues against the years ahead and keep score.

Start with 2030. April 11th is a Thursday. Holy Thursday that year falls a week later, on the 18th, so it's an ordinary Thursday. And April 11th is the feast of Saint Stanislaus, a bishop of Kraków, in Poland. In 1079, he was killed by the king while celebrating Mass. He died at the altar. Not Spanish. And to a girl in a Spanish mountain village in the 1960s, Stanislaus would have been a very strange name indeed. April. Thursday. Inside the window. A martyr of the Eucharist. Not Spanish. Not a great feast. Six for six.

But 2030 has an asterisk, and we promised honesty. Before 1969, Stanislaus was celebrated in May, not April. When Conchita was a girl, April 11th wasn't his day. Believers say God knew the calendar would change. Skeptics say that's convenient. Both of them have a point.

Next, 2032. Two Thursdays fall in the window, April 8th and April 15th. Neither is Holy Thursday. Neither falls inside the Easter octave. But on neither day does the Church keep the feast of a known martyr of the Eucharist. Some researchers say maybe the saint hasn't been canonized yet. That would explain why Conchita had never heard the name.

And now the date almost everyone has missed. 2034. You'll see it offered as the fix for 2028. Same day, April 13th, a Thursday, the feast of Saint Hermenegild. And this time, people say, it isn't Holy Thursday.

That's true. Holy Thursday in 2034 falls on April 6th. Which means Easter Sunday is April 9th. Which means April 13th, 2034 is the Thursday of the Easter octave, inside the eight days the Church celebrates as a single, great feast of the Lord. During that week, Hermenegild's feast isn't even kept.

So 2034 doesn't fix the problem of 2028. It keeps the Spanish problem and adds a great feast of the Lord. Two clues failed, again.

The scoreboard. 2026, gone. 2028, fails two. 2030, passes all six, with one asterisk. 2032, passes, if a saint we don't know yet is someday raised to the altars. 2034, fails two.

One year comes closest. But even it doesn't let us skip the Warning, the signs, or the eight days.

## 17:24 — [WHAT THE CHURCH SAYS]

And through all of this, we need to know where the Church stands.

The bishops of Santander, the diocese that includes Garabandal, have never found proof that the apparitions are supernatural. But they have never declared them false either. The question has been left open.

In 2024, Cardinal Víctor Manuel Fernández, who leads the Vatican's office for doctrine, spoke publicly in the same spirit. Mass may be celebrated in the village church, but not in connection with the apparitions.

That's not a no. It's a wait. And a Catholic can be at peace with that. We don't have to force an answer the Church hasn't given. We can watch, pray, and obey.
