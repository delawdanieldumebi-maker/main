PART ONE: THE MISSIONARY
============================================================

Marcel Lefebvre was born in 1905 in Tourcoing, a textile town in the far north of France, to a deeply Catholic family. Several of his siblings went into religious life. His father, a factory owner, later worked with the French Resistance and died in a German prison camp during the Second World War.

B-ROLL  (Shot 10, 02:05)
  1. Time card: "TOURCOING, FRANCE" / "1905"
  2. Archival footage: Period photos of northern French textile mills

Lefebvre was ordained a priest in 1929. A few years later he joined a missionary order called the Holy Ghost Fathers, and in 1932 they sent him to Gabon, then part of French Equatorial Africa. He stayed on the continent for most of the next three decades, and he rose quickly.

B-ROLL  (Shot 11, 02:26)
  1. Archival footage: Vintage photos of a 1930s African mission
  2. Archival footage: Spiritan missionaries. Search: Holy Ghost Fathers Gabon archive

In 1947 he became a bishop in Senegal. The following year, Pope Pius the Twelfth made him apostolic delegate for French-speaking Africa. An apostolic delegate is the pope's personal representative in a region, so Lefebvre was now effectively Rome's man for dozens of dioceses across the continent. In 1955 he became the first Archbishop of Dakar.

B-ROLL  (Shot 12, 02:45)
  1. Archival footage: Lefebvre in episcopal dress in Africa
  2. Aerial: Dakar cathedral wide shot

This matters for everything that follows. Marcel Lefebvre was not an outsider who wandered into a fight with Rome. He was one of the Church's most senior and trusted missionary bishops, and for most of his career he was the establishment.

============================================================
PART TWO: THE COUNCIL
============================================================

The Second Vatican Council was a gathering of more than two thousand bishops from around the world. It met in Rome in four sessions between 1962 and 1965, and it set out to rethink how the Church spoke to the modern world.

B-ROLL  (Shot 15, 03:35)
  1. Archival footage: Rows of mitred bishops in the nave of St. Peter's

What came out of it changed almost everything an ordinary Catholic could see. Within a few years, Mass was being said in local languages instead of Latin, and the priest usually faced the people instead of the altar. Catholics were encouraged to pray alongside Protestants. The Church's tone toward Judaism, Islam and other religions softened dramatically.

B-ROLL  (Shot 16, 03:51)
  1. Archival footage: 1960s parish Mass facing the people
  2. Archival footage: Ecumenical prayer service

Most bishops welcomed these changes. A minority fought them hard, and Lefebvre was one of their organizers. During the Council he helped lead a bloc of conservative bishops that tried to shape the documents from inside. They won some edits and lost the big votes.

B-ROLL  (Shot 17, 04:13)
  1. Archival footage: Council fathers debating, close shots of faces

Three issues bothered him most.

B-ROLL  (Shot 18, 04:31)
  1. Sacred art: Three candles on a dark altar

The first was the liturgy. To Lefebvre, the old Latin Mass wasn't just a style of worship. It was a precise statement of Catholic belief about sacrifice and priesthood, and changing the words meant changing the belief.

B-ROLL  (Shot 19, 04:33)
  1. Archival footage: Traditional Latin Mass, elevation of the Host

The second was ecumenism, the effort to build unity with other Christian churches. He saw it as blurring the line between the true Church and everyone else.

B-ROLL  (Shot 20, 04:48)
  1. Sacred art: Close shot of a stained-glass cross

The third, and the one he returned to more than any other, was religious liberty. The Council taught that no one should be coerced by the state in matters of religion. That sounds uncontroversial today. But nineteenth-century popes had written that error has no rights, and that a Catholic state had a duty to favor the true faith. Lefebvre read the new teaching as a direct contradiction of the old one. If the Church could reverse itself on that, he asked, what couldn't it reverse?

B-ROLL  (Shot 21, 04:58)
  1. Sacred art: Old papal documents or wax seals
  2. Quote card: CANON LAW TERM / "Religious liberty: no one to be coerced by the state in matters of religion."

That question is the root of the story. Lefebvre never said the Catholic Church was false. He said the Council had broken with it.

B-ROLL  (Shot 22, 05:32)
  1. Sacred art: Christ the King statue (user frame 1)

In 1969, Pope Paul the Sixth issued a new Order of the Mass, the one most Catholics attend today. By then Lefebvre had already resigned as head of his order, after its members voted for reforms he refused to carry out. He was sixty-three years old, and by his own account he expected to retire quietly.

B-ROLL  (Shot 23, 05:41)

  2. News/press: Paul VI portrait

That isn't what happened.

============================================================
PART THREE: A SEMINARY IN THE MOUNTAINS
============================================================

Soon after he stepped down, a handful of young men came to him asking for a traditional priestly formation, the kind they felt they could no longer find in reformed seminaries. Lefebvre set up a house for them in Fribourg, Switzerland.

B-ROLL  (Shot 25, 06:05)
  1. Aerial: Fribourg old town wide shot

On the first of November, 1970, the local bishop, François Charrière of Lausanne, Geneva and Fribourg, formally recognized the group as the Society of Saint Pius the Tenth. The name honored the early twentieth-century pope best known for his campaign against theological modernism.

B-ROLL  (Shot 26, 06:21)
  1. Time card: "1970"
  2. Archival footage: Early SSPX seminarians

The recognition itself was modest. The Society was set up as a "pious union," the lowest rung of official Church approval, and its rules were approved on a trial basis for six years. It was legal, provisional, and supervised by a local bishop.

B-ROLL  (Shot 27, 06:38)
  1. Quote card: CANON LAW TERM / "Pious union: the lowest rung of Church approval. Trial basis: six years."

Supporters gave the Society property in Écône, a small village in the Rhône valley in the canton of Valais. That became its seminary. Young men came from France, Switzerland, Germany, the United States and elsewhere, and the numbers grew quickly. That growth is exactly what started to worry Rome.

B-ROLL  (Shot 28, 06:55)
  1. Aerial: Drone over the Écône seminary and the Valais vineyards

In late 1974 the Vatican sent official visitors to inspect the seminary. Their visit went badly. A few weeks later, Lefebvre wrote a declaration that became the Society's founding creed. He said he adhered with all his heart to Catholic Rome, the Rome of tradition, and refused to follow what he called the Rome of neo-Modernist and neo-Protestant tendencies. In other words, he believed there were two Romes, and that he got to decide which one he was obeying.

B-ROLL  (Shot 29, 07:15)
  1. Time card: "1974"
  2. Sacred art: Split frame: St. Peter's dome and its reflection in water

Rome's answer was blunt. In 1975, with Vatican approval, the new local bishop withdrew the Society's recognition. On paper, the Society of Saint Pius the Tenth no longer existed.

B-ROLL  (Shot 30, 07:46)
  1. Sacred art: Close shot of a seal pressed on a document

Lefebvre kept going anyway.

B-ROLL  (Shot 31, 07:57)
  1. Archival footage: Lefebvre walking into the Écône chapel

On the twenty-ninth of June, 1976, he ordained a new class of priests at Écône despite explicit instructions not to. Pope Paul the Sixth responded by suspending him, which meant he was forbidden to exercise any priestly or episcopal ministry at all: no Mass, no sacraments, no ordinations.

B-ROLL  (Shot 32, 07:59)
  1. Time card: "1976"
  2. Archival footage: 1976 ordination footage
  3. Quote card: CANON LAW TERM / "Suspension a divinis: forbidden to exercise any sacred ministry."

A few weeks later, he said Mass in front of thousands of people in Lille. That September, Paul the Sixth received him in a private audience at Castel Gandolfo. Nothing changed.

B-ROLL  (Shot 33, 08:18)
  1. Archival footage: Lille 1976 crowd
  2. Aerial: Castel Gandolfo wide shot

For the next decade, the Society operated in a grey zone. It ordained priests without permission, opened priories and seminaries in several countries, and kept talking to Rome the whole time. Pope John Paul the Second, elected in 1978, reopened those talks. In 1984, Rome allowed limited use of the old Latin Mass with a local bishop's approval, partly to take the pressure off.

B-ROLL  (Shot 34, 08:30)
  1. Archival footage: SSPX priories montage
  2. News/press: John Paul II portrait

Then in 1986, John Paul the Second invited leaders of the world's religions to pray for peace in Assisi. For Lefebvre, that was the last straw. A pope sharing a stage with non-Christian religious leaders, as he saw it, was ecumenism carried to its logical end.

B-ROLL  (Shot 35, 08:55)
  1. Time card: "1986"
  2. Archival footage: Assisi 1986 interreligious meeting

The next year he announced that he intended to consecrate bishops.

B-ROLL  (Shot 36, 09:13)

============================================================
PART FOUR: THE PROBLEM ONLY A BISHOP CAN SOLVE
============================================================

To understand why that announcement was so explosive, you need to know one thing about how the Catholic Church works.

B-ROLL  (Shot 37, 09:18)
  1. Sacred art: Hands of a bishop, close shot

Only a bishop can ordain a priest. Only bishops can make new bishops. Catholics believe this chain of ordinations, called apostolic succession, runs back unbroken to the apostles. Without a bishop, a seminary is just a school. It can train as many men as it likes, but none of them can ever be ordained.

B-ROLL  (Shot 38, 09:26)
  1. Sacred art: Fresco of the apostles
  2. Archival footage: Laying on of hands at an ordination

By 1987 the Society had seminaries on several continents and hundreds of students, and exactly one bishop willing to ordain them: Marcel Lefebvre, who was eighty-one.

B-ROLL  (Shot 39, 09:47)
  1. Archival footage: Elderly Lefebvre, slow push-in

If he died without a successor, the whole project would stop producing priests. The existing ones would carry on for a while, and then, over a generation, the Society would simply age out of existence.

B-ROLL  (Shot 40, 09:57)
  1. Sacred art: Empty seminary corridor, candle burning down

Rome understood this as well as he did, and that is what gave the negotiations their tension. Each side knew the clock was on Lefebvre's side of the table.

B-ROLL  (Shot 41, 10:11)
  1. Sacred art: Close shot of a church tower clock

In late 1987, the Vatican sent Cardinal Édouard Gagnon to carry out a formal inspection of the Society's houses. By most accounts he came back impressed. Serious negotiations followed in the spring of 1988. On Rome's side they were led by Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, then head of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, the man who would later become Pope Benedict the Sixteenth.

B-ROLL  (Shot 42, 10:22)
  1. News/press: Gagnon portrait

PART FIVE: THE TWENTY-FOUR HOURS
============================================================

On the fifth of May, 1988, Lefebvre and Ratzinger signed a protocol of agreement.

B-ROLL  (Shot 43, 10:48)
  1. Time card: "MAY 5" / "1988"

This part of the story usually gets compressed to a single line, so it's worth slowing down, because the terms were remarkable.

B-ROLL  (Shot 44, 10:54)
  1. Sacred art: Fountain pen over parchment

Under the protocol, the Society would be regularized as an official body within the Church. It could keep the old liturgy. A Roman commission would be created to handle its affairs, with members of the Society sitting on it. The theological disputes over the Council would be dealt with through continued study, not ultimatums. And the Holy See agreed in principle that a member of the Society would be consecrated a bishop.

B-ROLL  (Shot 45, 11:02)
  1. Quote card: THE MAY 5 PROTOCOL. Build the terms one by one: official recognition, the old liturgy, a Roman commission, one bishop from the Society

In exchange, Lefebvre signed a doctrinal declaration accepting the Pope's authority and committing to a respectful attitude toward the Council's teaching.

B-ROLL  (Shot 46, 11:31)
  1. Sacred art: A signature line on a document

It wasn't everything he wanted. But it solved the problem that had driven him for a decade: the Society would get a bishop of its own, with Rome's blessing.

B-ROLL  (Shot 47, 11:39)
  1. Archival footage: Écône chapel exterior

He signed on the fifth.

B-ROLL  (Shot 48, 11:50)
  1. Time card: "MAY 5"

On the sixth, he took it back.

B-ROLL  (Shot 49, 11:52)
  1. Time card: "MAY 6", hard cut

In a letter to Ratzinger the next day, Lefebvre wrote that he had signed in good faith, but said he could no longer wait on Rome's timing. The protocol promised a bishop but gave no date. It also didn't guarantee that the man chosen would be the one Lefebvre wanted. He demanded a firm date, the thirtieth of June, and said that if Rome refused, he would proceed without permission.

B-ROLL  (Shot 50, 11:55)
  1. Sacred art: Envelope and wax seal
  2. Quote card: LEFEBVRE'S DEMAND / "A firm date: June 30."

What changed overnight? Lefebvre's own later explanation was distrust. In the hours after signing, he became convinced that Rome was playing for time: that without a fixed date, the consecration would be delayed and delayed again until he died, and the Society would be absorbed on Rome's terms. He was eighty-two. He had watched Rome move slowly all his life. He decided he could not afford to trust it one more time.

B-ROLL  (Shot 51, 12:23)
  1. Archival footage: Lefebvre in profile, at a window if available. Slow drift

Ratzinger didn't slam the door. Within weeks he offered a concrete date, the fifteenth of August, the Feast of the Assumption, only six weeks later than the one Lefebvre had demanded. He asked for more candidate files so a choice could be made.

B-ROLL  (Shot 52, 12:51)
  1. News/press: Ratzinger
  2. Quote card: "June 30 vs. August 15" with the six-week gap shown

Lefebvre didn't take it. On the second of June he wrote to John Paul the Second directly, announcing that he would go ahead. The Pope wrote back urging him not to. On the seventeenth of June, the Vatican issued a formal canonical warning spelling out exactly what would happen if he did.

B-ROLL  (Shot 53, 13:08)
  1. Time card: "JUNE 1988"
  2. News/press: John Paul II

So the final break didn't come down to doctrine. The man had already signed off on the doctrine. It came down to a gap of about six weeks, and whether he could bring himself to trust the other side for that long.

============================================================
PART SIX: VALID, BUT NOT ALLOWED
============================================================

Before we get to what happened next, there's a distinction in Catholic law that the whole story hinges on, and it's one most people get wrong.

B-ROLL  (Shot 55, 13:45)
  1. Sacred art: Canon law book, pages turning

When a bishop makes someone a bishop, Church law asks two separate questions.

B-ROLL  (Shot 56, 13:56)
  1. Quote card: Two columns, "VALID" and "LICIT"

The first is whether it's valid: did the sacrament actually happen? If the man doing the consecrating is a real bishop, uses the right words and actions, and means to do what the Church does, the answer is yes. The new man is a bishop, permanently.

B-ROLL  (Shot 57, 14:01)
  1. Quote card: "VALID" column highlighted
  2. Sacred art: Chrism oil and a mitre

The second is whether it's licit: was it permitted? For a bishop, permission means a pontifical mandate, a direct authorization from the pope.

B-ROLL  (Shot 58, 14:19)
  1. Quote card: "LICIT" column highlighted: pontifical mandate

The two questions are independent. A consecration can be completely real and completely forbidden at the same time. Think of a key cut from the original mold. It will open the door every time. Whether you were allowed to open the door is a separate question, and the lock doesn't care.

B-ROLL  (Shot 59, 14:28)
  1. Sacred art: Old iron key turning in a church door lock

Rome never claimed Lefebvre's consecrations would be fake. That was precisely the danger. They would be real bishops, able to ordain real priests, operating entirely outside the Pope's authority.

B-ROLL  (Shot 60, 14:48)
  1. Aerial: St. Peter's dome

Lefebvre had an answer ready. Church law itself recognizes that a person acting out of genuine necessity can be excused from a penalty or have it reduced. The provisions are canons thirteen twenty-three and thirteen twenty-four. Lefebvre argued that the Church was in a state of emergency: the faith itself was in danger, and he was saving the priesthood for future generations. In that situation, he said, the law couldn't bind him.

B-ROLL  (Shot 61, 15:00)
  1. Quote card: CODE OF CANON LAW / "Canons 1323–1324: necessity can excuse or reduce a penalty."

Rome's reply was that a state of necessity can't be declared by the person it would excuse, and certainly not against the explicit judgment of the pope.

B-ROLL  (Shot 62, 15:28)
  1. Sacred art: Papal keys emblem carved in stone

Both sides were about to act on their own reading.

B-ROLL  (Shot 63, 15:39)
  1. Aerial: Dusk over the Valais

PART SEVEN: THE THIRTIETH OF JUNE, 1988
============================================================

On the thirtieth of June, 1988, under a vast tent in a field at Écône, Marcel Lefebvre consecrated four priests of the Society as bishops.

B-ROLL  (Shot 64, 15:43)
  1. Time card: "JUNE 30" / "1988"
  2. Archival footage: The 1988 tent at Écône, wide

Standing with him as co-consecrator was Antônio de Castro Mayer, the eighty-four-year-old retired bishop of Campos in Brazil, the only other Catholic bishop in the world willing to take part.

B-ROLL  (Shot 65, 15:52)
  1. News/press: Castro Mayer portrait

The four new bishops were Bernard Tissier de Mallerais, a Frenchman; Richard Williamson, an Englishman and a convert; Alfonso de Galarreta, a Spaniard raised in Argentina, aged thirty-one; and Bernard Fellay, a Swiss priest aged thirty. For a while, Fellay was the youngest Catholic bishop in the world.

B-ROLL  (Shot 66, 16:04)
  1. News/press portrait (1988): Tissier de Mallerais
  2. News/press portrait (1988): Williamson
  3. News/press portrait (1988): de Galarreta
  4. News/press portrait (1988): Fellay (hold)

In his sermon, Lefebvre gave the ceremony a name. He called it "Operation Survival." To him it wasn't rebellion but a rescue.

B-ROLL  (Shot 67, 16:23)
  1. Archival footage: Lefebvre preaching
  2. Quote card: LEFEBVRE, JUNE 30, 1988 / "Operation Survival."

Rome didn't see it that way. The very next day, the first of July, Cardinal Bernardin Gantin, head of the Congregation for Bishops, issued a decree. It declared that all six men, Lefebvre, de Castro Mayer and the four new bishops, had incurred excommunication.

B-ROLL  (Shot 68, 16:32)
  1. Time card: "JULY 1" / "1988"
  2. News/press: Gantin portrait

Notice the wording: had incurred. Gantin wasn't imposing a penalty. Under the Code of Canon Law then in force, consecrating a bishop without a papal mandate carried an automatic excommunication, for both the consecrating bishop and the man consecrated. The provision was canon thirteen eighty-two, together with canon thirteen sixty-four, which covers schism. The decree only confirmed, in writing, something that had already happened the moment the ceremony ended.

B-ROLL  (Shot 69, 16:49)
  1. Quote card: CODE OF CANON LAW (1983) / "Canon 1382, Canon 1364". "had incurred" underlined in oxblood

A day later, on the second of July, John Paul the Second went further. In a letter called Ecclesia Dei, he described the consecrations as a schismatic act, a break with the unity of the Church. He also warned ordinary Catholics that formally adhering to that break carried the same penalty.

B-ROLL  (Shot 70, 17:16)
  1. Time card: "JULY 2" / "1988"
  2. News/press: John Paul II

So on the evening of the thirtieth of June, Bernard Fellay and Alfonso de Galarreta walked out of that tent as fully real bishops. They could ordain priests, confirm children and consecrate other bishops, and every one of those sacraments would be valid. But in the eyes of the Church whose sacraments they were, they had no right to perform any of them.

B-ROLL  (Shot 71, 17:37)
  1. Archival footage: Fellay and de Galarreta in 1988 vestments, slow push-in

That contradiction would define the Society for the next thirty-eight years.

PART EIGHT: THE ROAD NOT TAKEN
============================================================

That's where most versions of this story move on. But in the summer of 1988, some members of the Society made a different choice, and it's the most important counterpoint to everything the Society says about itself.

B-ROLL  (Shot 73, 18:06)
  1. Sacred art: A path forking in a monastery garden

Not every priest of the Society followed Lefebvre into the consecrations. About a dozen of them held to the agreement he'd signed and then abandoned. They wanted the same things he did: the old Latin Mass, traditional seminary training, the theology they'd grown up with. They just weren't willing to break with the Pope to keep them.

B-ROLL  (Shot 74, 18:20)
  1. Archival footage: FSSP priests at a Latin Mass

On the eighteenth of July, 1988, less than three weeks after the consecrations, they met at a Cistercian abbey in Switzerland and founded a new community: the Priestly Fraternity of Saint Peter. The name was not subtle. Peter is the apostle Catholics see as the first pope.

B-ROLL  (Shot 75, 18:43)
  1. Time card: "JULY 18" / "1988"
  2. Aerial: Hauterive Abbey wide shot

That October, Rome gave them full canonical recognition. They were granted most of what Lefebvre's protocol had offered, inside the structure of the Church from day one.

B-ROLL  (Shot 76, 19:01)
  1. Sacred art: Papal seal on a document

Today the Fraternity has more than three hundred and fifty priests working in dioceses around the world.

B-ROLL  (Shot 77, 19:12)
  1. Archival footage: FSSP ordination montage

That doesn't settle the theological argument. But it does undercut one central claim. The Society has always said the old rite could only be preserved by breaking with Rome. A dozen of Lefebvre's own priests tested that claim within weeks, and it turned out not to be true. They just weren't the ones in charge.

============================================================
PART NINE: THE LONG MIDDLE
============================================================

Marcel Lefebvre died on the twenty-fifth of March, 1991, still excommunicated. Bishop de Castro Mayer died a month later.

B-ROLL  (Shot 79, 19:41)
  1. Time card: "1991"
  2. Archival footage: Lefebvre's tomb at Écône

The Society didn't collapse. It grew. In 1994 its members elected Bernard Fellay, the thirty-year-old from the tent at Écône, as superior general. He would lead the Society for twenty-four years, until 2018. Alfonso de Galarreta rose beside him and eventually became first assistant, effectively his second in command.

B-ROLL  (Shot 80, 19:48)
  1. Time card: "1994"
  2. News/press: Fellay as superior general
  3. News/press: de Galarreta

Under Fellay, the Society's position hardened into a strategy. It would talk to Rome, but it set two conditions before any real reconciliation: every priest in the world must be free to celebrate the old Latin Mass, and the 1988 excommunications must be withdrawn.

B-ROLL  (Shot 81, 20:07)
  1. Quote card: THE SOCIETY'S CONDITIONS / "1. Free the old Mass. 2. Withdraw the excommunications."

Then a new pope met both conditions.

B-ROLL  (Shot 82, 20:25)
  1. News/press: Benedict XVI on the loggia

In 2007, Benedict the Sixteenth, the same Joseph Ratzinger who had signed the protocol with Lefebvre nineteen years earlier, issued a document called Summorum Pontificum. It said the old Latin Mass had never been abolished and that any priest could celebrate it without asking a bishop.

B-ROLL  (Shot 83, 20:28)
  1. Time card: "2007"
  2. Archival footage: Latin Mass in a parish
  3. Quote card: Condition 1 crossed off the conditions card

In December 2008, Fellay wrote asking for the excommunications to be lifted. On the twenty-first of January, 2009, Benedict granted it. A decree from the Congregation for Bishops lifted the penalty on the four surviving bishops. It said explicitly that this was meant as a first step toward full communion.

B-ROLL  (Shot 84, 20:46)

============================================================
PART ELEVEN: ONE WORD
============================================================

"Excommunicated" is a word people use loosely, so it's worth being exact about what happened in 2026 and why.

B-ROLL  (Shot 107, 26:50)
  1. Sacred art: Canon law book

Church law was reorganized in 2021, and the offense of consecrating a bishop without a pontifical mandate moved to a new number: canon thirteen eighty-seven. The rule itself didn't change. The bishop who consecrates and the man who receives the consecration both incur excommunication automatically. Canon lawyers call this a latae sententiae penalty, which roughly means a sentence already passed. No trial, no hearing, no judge. The penalty attaches at the moment of the act.

B-ROLL  (Shot 108, 26:58)
  1. Quote card: CODE OF CANON LAW (2021 REVISION) / "Canon 1387"
  2. Quote card: LATIN / "Latae sententiae: 'a sentence already passed.'"

So the decree that followed in 2026, like Cardinal Gantin's in 1988, didn't excommunicate anyone. It recorded an excommunication that canon law says had already happened.

B-ROLL  (Shot 109, 27:28)
  1. News/press: Side by side: Gantin 1988 and Fernández 2026

In 2026, the Dicastery applied canon thirteen eighty-seven, together with canon thirteen sixty-four on schism, to Bishop de Galarreta and the four new bishops. Bishop Fellay's case rested on a different footing: canon thirteen sixty-four alone, for publicly adhering to a schismatic act as the assisting bishop.

B-ROLL  (Shot 110, 27:38)
  1. Quote card: Name-and-canon grid: de Galarreta and the 4 new bishops: 1387 + 1364 §1, Fellay: 1364 §1

That brings us to the word that actually decides all of this. The Church's definition of schism is in canon seven fifty-one: the withdrawal of submission to the Supreme Pontiff, or of communion with the members of the Church subject to him.

B-ROLL  (Shot 111, 27:56)
  1. Quote card: CANON 751 / "Schism is the withdrawal of submission to the Supreme Pontiff, or of communion with the members of the Church subject to him."

Look at what that definition doesn't include.

B-ROLL  (Shot 112, 28:13)
  1. Quote card: Same card, everything dims except "submission"

It doesn't say "denying a doctrine." It doesn't say "teaching error." Schism isn't heresy. A person can hold every single article of the Catholic faith, exactly as it was taught in 1950, and still be in schism. The only thing the definition requires is refusing submission.

B-ROLL  (Shot 113, 28:16)
  1. Sacred art: Christ the King statue (user frame 1)

That reframes the entire forty-year dispute. The Society has always argued about substance: whether the Council contradicted earlier popes, whether the new Mass weakened Catholic belief, whether religious liberty broke with tradition. They may be right about some of it, and plenty of loyal Catholic theologians have raised similar questions.

B-ROLL  (Shot 114, 28:34)
  1. Archival footage: Montage: 1962 Council, 1988 tent, 2026 procession

But canonically, none of that was ever the charge. The charge was about who has the final word on whether the Council got it right. In 1988, and again in 2026, the Society's answer was the same: not Rome, or at least not yet.

B-ROLL  (Shot 115, 28:54)
  1. Aerial: St. Peter's dome, slow push-in

Pagliarani's family image works both ways. A family does share a name and a home. But in the Catholic understanding of it, the family also has a father, and the question was never whether the Society loved the house. It was whether they'd accept that the house had someone at its head.
