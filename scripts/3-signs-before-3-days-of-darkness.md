## [1:33–2:14] WHY WARN AT ALL?  ·  116 words

Let's start with the word "why," because it's the question nobody asks.

If God means to send a darkness, why announce it? Why give signs at all? A storm doesn't send a letter before it comes.

The answer runs through every one of these messages, and it's simple. The signs are not the punishment. The signs are the mercy. Each one is a door held open a little longer.

A father who loves his children doesn't just lock the house at sundown. He walks out onto the porch and calls their names. Once. Twice. A third time.

Three signs. Three calls home.

## [2:14–4:42] SIGN ONE: THE EARTH  ·  425 words

The first sign is the one the saints described with the most detail and the least drama. It doesn't arrive in a single day. It unfolds over years. And it happens here, on the ground.

Blessed Anna Maria Taigi lived in Rome in the early 1800s. She was a wife and the mother of seven, running a crowded household on a butler's wages. The people who knew her said that for most of her adult life she saw a small, bright light in front of her, like a tiny sun. And in that light she saw things far away, and things that had not happened yet.

She said God would send two punishments. One would come from heaven. But first, one would come from the earth. Wars. Revolutions. Great evils that men would bring down on themselves.

Notice the order. Before heaven acts, the earth falls apart. And it falls apart by our own hands.

In that same city, another woman was being shown almost the same thing.

Her name was Elisabetta Canori Mora. She was born in Rome in 1774 to a comfortable family. She married a young lawyer named Cristoforo Mora. And within a few years, the marriage was a ruin. He was jealous. Then he was unfaithful. Then he spent the family's money on another woman, until they lost almost everything they had.

Her own relatives begged her to leave him. She refused. She took in work to put food on the table. She raised her two daughters. And she prayed for her husband every single day, for years, without ever seeing a change.

And in that prayer, she saw things. According to the accounts of her visions, she saw the world shaken by unbelief and rebellion. She saw the Church attacked from the outside and abandoned from the inside. She saw people turning away from God, not in anger, but in indifference. And she saw that a great purification was coming.

That's the first sign. Not one event. A season. Violence spreading. Faith pushed out of public life. Families coming apart. And nature itself seeming to turn against us. Floods, fires, droughts, and sicknesses that arrive faster than we can name them.

## [4:42–6:12] WHY TRUST THESE WOMEN?  ·  256 words

Now, a fair question. Why should anyone listen to two Roman housewives from two hundred years ago?

Because of what happened when they were tested.

Anna Maria Taigi's predictions weren't all about the end of the world. Most of them were small, specific, and checkable. Those who knew her recorded that she named events in families, in Rome, and in the Church before they happened. Cardinals came to her small apartment for advice. And after she died in 1837, the Church opened her cause, studied her life for decades, and in 1920 declared her blessed.

Elisabetta's story has an ending you can check too. It's said she told those close to her that after her death, her husband would come back to God. And not only that. He would become a priest.

It sounded absurd. Cristoforo was the last man in Rome anyone would have picked.

Elisabetta died in 1825. Her death shook Cristoforo to the core. He confessed. He repented. He joined the Franciscans. And he was ordained a priest. The man who had betrayed her spent the rest of his life at the altar. In 1994, Pope John Paul the Second declared Elisabetta blessed.

Two women. Both married. Both mothers. Both living in hard homes. Both examined by the Church and raised to the altars. And both pointing to the same order of events.

## [6:12–8:04] SIGN TWO: THE SOUL  ·  322 words

The second sign is the one almost nobody connects to the darkness. And it's the most personal of the three.

In the 1960s, in the mountain village of Garabandal in northern Spain, four young girls said the Virgin Mary told them about something called the Warning. In one moment, every person on earth, wherever they are and whatever they're doing, will see their own soul the way God sees it. Every sin. Every good thing left undone. Every time we said no to Him.

One of the seers called it a correction of the conscience of the world. She said it would be frightening. And she said that after it, it would be very hard for anyone to say that God doesn't exist.

Now, I need to be careful here. Garabandal is a separate set of messages from the ones about the Three Days of Darkness. The Church has never approved it, and it has never condemned it. But many Catholics who study these things see the same pattern running through both. Before the outer darkness, an inner light. Before the sky goes black, heaven shows every person the truth about their own heart.

It would be a last examination of conscience, given to the whole human race at once.

Marie-Julie Jahenny, the French mystic we're about to meet, spoke of something in the same spirit. She said that before the darkness, God would give a final time of grace. A last call. So that no one could ever say they were not warned.

That's the second sign. Not a storm. Not a war. A moment of truth. And as far as anyone can tell, it hasn't happened yet.

## [8:04–9:35] THE WITNESS IN THE FARMHOUSE  ·  259 words

The third sign comes from the woman who described the Three Days of Darkness in more detail than anyone else. So before we hear it, you need to know who she was.

Marie-Julie Jahenny was born in 1850 in Brittany, in the west of France, to a family of poor farmers. She barely went to school. At twenty-three, wounds opened in her hands, her feet, and her side. The wounds of Christ. Later, a ring of wounds appeared around her head, like a crown of thorns.

Her bishop didn't simply take her word for it. He sent doctors. One of them, Doctor Antoine Imbert-Gourbeyre, was a professor of medicine who spent his career studying people who claimed the stigmata. He'd seen frauds. He knew exactly what to look for. He examined Marie-Julie over a long period. He came away convinced the wounds were real, and he defended her for the rest of his life.

She spent almost her whole life in one small farmhouse in a hamlet called La Fraudais. For a long stretch of years, she was kept away from the sacraments because of a dispute that wasn't her doing. Imagine that. A woman who lived for the Eucharist, cut off from it. She accepted it without one word of protest. She died in 1941, at ninety-one, in that same small house.

The Church has never formally ruled on her messages. But her life, the suffering, the obedience, the silence, is not the life of someone chasing attention.

And what she said about the last sign is extraordinary.

## [9:35–11:09] SIGN THREE: THE SKY  ·  269 words

The third sign is the one that ends the waiting. It's in the sky.

Marie-Julie described the heavens changing in a way no one on earth could ignore. A sky heavy and red, like fire. Thunder that does not stop. Lightning that frightens even those inside their homes. And the sun itself losing its light.

She also described the darkness with a strange precision. Three days and three nights, she said, falling on a Thursday, a Friday, and a Saturday. Think about those days. Thursday, the Last Supper. Friday, the Cross. Saturday, the silence of the tomb. The same three days as the Passion of Christ.

And she was told that in those days, nothing made by human hands would give light. Only candles of blessed wax would burn.

If that sounds like something from the edge of Christianity, open the Gospel of Luke, chapter twenty-one. Jesus Himself says there will be signs in the sun, the moon, and the stars, and on earth, nations in anguish. And the prophet Joel, quoted by Saint Peter on the day of Pentecost, says the sun will be turned to darkness before the great day of the Lord.

The saints didn't invent the sign in the sky. They were echoing Scripture.

But here is what matters most. None of them gave a date. Not Anna Maria. Not Elisabetta. Not Marie-Julie. Anyone online who tells you the year, the month, or the day is claiming something the saints themselves refused to say. The day and the hour belong to God alone.

What they gave us instead were the signs. So let's count them.

## [11:56–12:53] AN HONEST WORD ABOUT PADRE PIO  ·  163 words

Before we go any further, there's something you'll see all over the internet, and it deserves an honest word.

There's a famous message about the Three Days of Darkness that's usually credited to Padre Pio. It tells people to cover their windows, to pray, to stay inside. It's usually dated to around 1950. You've probably seen it shared a hundred times.

Here's the problem. There's no reliable evidence that Padre Pio ever wrote it. No original has ever been produced. Serious researchers of his life treat it as unverified at best, and many consider it falsely attributed.

Why does that matter? Because the women who did speak about the darkness lived tested, examined lives. When we pass a message along just because it has a famous name on it, we don't strengthen the real warning. We weaken it.

So here's a simple rule. Hold on to what's reliable. Set aside what isn't. And never let a rumor take the place of a sacrament.

## [12:53–14:07] WHAT THEY ASKED US TO DO  ·  212 words

So what did the saints actually ask of us?

Surprisingly little. Nothing about bunkers, buying gold, or stockpiling a year of food. What they asked for, you could carry in one hand.

A blessed wax candle. Not a battery candle. Not a scented one from the mall. Real wax, blessed by a priest. If you don't have one, bring one to your parish after Mass and ask. It takes a priest less than a minute. And every year on February second, the Feast of the Presentation, the day the old Catholics called Candlemas, the Church blesses candles at Mass.

A crucifix. Holy water. A rosary. A blessed image of Our Lord or Our Lady on the wall.

And for the days themselves, the instructions are just as simple. Close the doors. Cover the windows. Stay inside. Pray. And above all, do not look outside, no matter what you hear.

Marie-Julie warned that there would be sounds out there. Voices. Even voices that sound like people you love, calling you to open the door. You don't open it. You keep praying.

And if you'll be alone during those days, you are not less protected. Protection doesn't depend on how many people are in a house. It depends on Who the house belongs to.

## [14:07–15:49] WHY NOT LOOK OUTSIDE?  ·  292 words

Now, the detail I promised you at the beginning. Why did the saints insist, over and over, do not look outside?

Most people assume it's a punishment for curiosity. But go back to Scripture, and you'll find this exact instruction has been given before. Twice.

The first time was the night of the very first Passover. God told the Israelites to mark their doorposts with the blood of the lamb. And then He said this, in Exodus chapter twelve: "None of you shall go out of the door of his house until the morning." That night, death passed through Egypt. And the houses that stayed shut, under the sign of the lamb, were passed over.

The second time was at Sodom. The angels pulled Lot and his family out of the city, and gave them one command. Do not look back. Lot's wife turned around. Just to see. And she never moved again.

Do you see the pattern? In both stories, the closed door and the eyes turned away aren't about fear. They're about trust. The question was never, "What's happening out there?" The question was, "Do you believe the One who told you to stay inside?"

That's what the saints were telling us. When the darkness comes, the protection isn't the walls. It isn't even the candle. It's the trust. A family praying behind a closed door, not even peeking through the curtain, is a family saying to God, "We don't need to see. We know You."

And in Exodus chapter ten, during the plague of darkness over Egypt, there's one line almost everyone skips. The darkness was so thick it could be felt. But all the children of Israel had light in their homes.

Same darkness outside. Different light inside.

## [15:49–17:10] THE CANDLE IS A SIGN, NOT A CHARM  ·  232 words

That brings us to the most important thing these women said. And it's the reason this is not a story about fear.

According to Marie-Julie, the blessed candles would not burn for everyone. In homes where God had been shut out, the same wax, with the same blessing, would refuse to light.

Let that sink in. The candle was never a magic object. It was never the protection. It was the sign of something already true. A house that belonged to God before the darkness ever came.

## [17:10–17:44] WHAT THE CHURCH SAYS  ·  98 words

To be fair and clear: the Church has never officially ruled on the Three Days of Darkness. These are private revelations. The Catechism teaches that private revelations don't add anything to the faith, and no Catholic is required to believe them. But the Church allows the faithful to find in them a help to live the Gospel more fully, as long as they don't contradict it.

And nothing these women asked for contradicts the Gospel. Pray. Repent. Trust. Stay close to the sacraments. Love your family. That's not a strange new teaching. It's the oldest teaching there is.

## [17:44–18:34] THE MORNING AFTER  ·  142 words

And here is the part that makes all of this make sense. What comes after.

Every one of these women spoke about the end of the darkness with joy.

Anna Maria Taigi said that afterward there would be conversions beyond counting, and that whole nations that had wandered from the Church for centuries would come home.

Elisabetta Canori Mora, according to her writings, saw the Church renewed. She saw Saint Peter himself come down to restore what had been broken, and a new springtime of faith across the world.

And Marie-Julie was told that after the purification, Christ would reign, and His reign would be a reign of peace.

So the darkness isn't the last word. It's the night before a morning. Just like Good Friday and Holy Saturday were not the end of the story. Thursday, Friday, Saturday. And then, Sunday.
