In July of this year, people came to a Carmelite church in Kildare, in Ireland, to stand for a few seconds beside the bones of a woman who died in 1897. She was twenty-four. She had spent the last nine years of her life behind the walls of one small convent in Normandy.

## 00:58 — THE PROMISE

The 17th of July, 1897. Thérèse has been in the convent infirmary for nine days. Tuberculosis is destroying her lungs. Beside the bed sits her older sister Pauline, known in the convent as Mother Agnes of Jesus, with a small notebook, writing down what the dying nun says.

That afternoon Thérèse tells her that she feels her mission is about to begin. And then she says the sentence that will travel around the world: "I want to spend my heaven doing good on earth."

You have probably seen that line on a holy card. Millions of people know it by heart.

But that was not the end of what she said. A few lines later, in the same notebook, on the same day, she added something else. A condition. And that condition decides whether her promise is still running tonight, or whether it ran out a long time ago.

I will show you that line. But first you need to know what it cost her to say any of it, because the woman who made this promise was in far more trouble than the holy cards let on.

## 02:38 — THE DARK

She was born Thérèse Martin in 1873, in Alençon, the youngest of nine children. When she was four, her mother, Zélie, died of breast cancer. At fifteen, after appeals that went all the way to the Pope, she entered the Carmel of Lisieux, where two of her sisters already lived. She never came out again.

Inside, she found what she called her little way. She was too small, she said, to climb to God by heroic deeds. So she would let God carry her, like a child carried up the stairs. Small things done with love. That was all.

It sounds sweet until you see it up close. There was an old, sick sister in the community who was hard to please, and every evening someone had to help her walk from the chapel to the dining room. Nobody wanted the job. Thérèse took it, and did it so cheerfully that the old nun never guessed what it cost her. There was another sister whose company she found almost unbearable, so she decided to treat her as if she were the person she loved most in the world. That was the little way. Not visions. Patience, done in secret, every day.

Then came the night of Holy Thursday, 1896. She went to bed, and she coughed up blood. In France in the 1890s, everybody knew what that meant.

But the blood was not the worst of it. Within days, something happened to her soul. The heaven she had believed in all her life, the heaven she had given up everything for, went dark. When she reached for it, she found nothing. She wrote that a voice in the darkness mocked her, telling her that death would bring her not heaven, but nothingness.

That darkness never lifted. It was still there in July of 1897, when she made her promise.

So here is the first problem. The woman who promised to spend her heaven on earth could no longer feel that heaven existed. Was her promise faith? Or was it the last hope of a frightened, dying girl?

She answered that herself. She wrote that in that dark year she made more acts of faith than in all the rest of her life. She did not promise because she could see. She promised because she chose to trust.

## 05:14 — THE FIRST MAN

Now, about the man in the hospital ward.

On the 15th of October, 1895, a letter came to the Carmel from a twenty-one-year-old seminarian named Maurice Bellière. He hoped to become a missionary, and he asked the prioress for a nun who would pray for him. The prioress was Thérèse's own sister Pauline. She gave the job to Thérèse.

Thérèse's two brothers had died as babies. Now she had one. She prayed for Maurice, she wrote to him, and she took him seriously.

A year later she was given a second brother, a young priest named Adolphe Roulland, who was being sent to China. In July of 1896, just after his ordination, he came to the convent and spoke with her through the grille of the parlour. It was the only time she ever saw either of them. A few weeks later, as he sailed for the province of Sichuan, she painted a picture for him and dated it the 20th of August, 1896.

And it was to these two young men, not to the world, that she first made her promise. On the 14th of July, 1897, three days before the notebook entry, she wrote to Father Roulland that she was counting on not staying idle in heaven. She wanted to go on working for the Church and for souls. In 2023, Pope Francis quoted the same letter to the whole Church: she wanted to save souls even after her death.

The very last letter she ever wrote, on the 10th of August, went to Maurice.

On the 30th of September, 1897, holding a crucifix, she said, "My God, I love you," and she died.

Remember Maurice's name. We will come back to him, and when we do, it will not be easy to hear.

## 07:05 — ON TRIAL

That should have been the end. A young nun dies. Her sisters mourn. Her notebooks go in a drawer.

Instead, something started that nobody at Lisieux had planned, and it put her promise on trial.

A year after her death, the convent printed her memoirs as a small book called Story of a Soul. Readers passed it to people who had never heard of Lisieux. It was reprinted again and again. Letters began pouring into the convent by the thousand, from people who said they had prayed to her and something had happened. In the First World War, soldiers carried her picture into the trenches.

But the Church does not run on letters. When Rome looked at her, it took nobody's word for it. The sisters who had sat at her bedside were questioned under oath. Doctors examined the cures people claimed. Two had to be accepted before she could be declared blessed, in 1923. Two more before she could be declared a saint, in 1925.

Rome even had her grave opened, in 1910, as the law of the Church required. Some people expected a body untouched by death. The record says plainly that only her bones were found. If you have ever read online that Thérèse is incorrupt, the people who opened her coffin said otherwise. Her cause did not rest on her body. It rested on her life, and on what kept happening after it.

Her promise passed. Two years later, this nun who never left her convent was named patron of all the Catholic missions in the world. And in 1997 she was named a Doctor of the Church.

By every official measure, the promise was being kept.

But there is one person that record never mentions. The first young man she ever promised to help. And while the whole world was discovering her, his life was falling apart.

## 09:02 — THE WARD IN CAEN

Maurice Bellière did become a missionary priest in Africa. Then his health broke down. He came home to Normandy very ill, with an illness that attacked his nerves and his mind.

On the 8th of June, 1907, he was admitted to the Bon Sauveur hospital in Caen.

Now listen to this. The Bon Sauveur in Caen was the same hospital where Thérèse's own father, Louis Martin, had been cared for in the years his mind failed. Thérèse had prayed for her father from behind convent walls while he lay in that place. It was the deepest grief of her life.

And now the brother she had promised to help lay in the very same place.

Maurice died there on the 14th of July, 1907. He was thirty-three years old, and he died in Caen, the city where he had been born. It was ten years, almost to the week, since she had promised to spend her heaven doing good on earth.

Here is what the record shows. She never promised Maurice an easy life. She never promised to take the hospital away. What she promised was that she would not be idle in heaven. That she would keep working for souls.

And the one place on earth where her own family had suffered most was exactly the place her brother ended up, as if she had walked that ground before him, so that he would not walk it alone.

Nobody can prove she was there. But it matches her words exactly. And if her promise can survive that, it can survive almost anything.

Except, perhaps, the one thing that has done it the most damage. Not suffering. Us.

## 11:03 — THE MYTH

You have probably seen the rose novena. Pray it for nine days, the card says, and if Thérèse hears you, she will send you a rose.

Here is the record. That novena did not come from Thérèse. It was started in December of 1925 by a Jesuit priest, Father Putigan, who asked for a rose as his own private sign. It is a beautiful devotion. But Thérèse never promised anyone a rose on demand, and the Church has never promised it either.

So here is the next danger. If part of the story is a later addition, is the whole thing a myth?

No. And this is the difference that matters. The roses on the cards came later. The promise did not. It is in her sister's notebook, dated the 17th of July, 1897, and it is in her own letter to Father Roulland. Strip away every holy card ever printed, and the promise is still standing.

The roses were only ever her picture for the good she would do. They were never a receipt.

## 12:09 — RIGHT NOW

And the strongest evidence was never a rose. It is the people who keep coming.

A young woman from Skopje joined a missionary order and chose her religious name after the nun of Lisieux. The world came to know her as Mother Teresa of Calcutta.

Think about who stands in those lines. Not tourists. A father with a son in prison. A widow. A nurse on her day off. A grandmother with one name on her lips. People who heard about her from someone, who heard about her from someone else, all the way back to a notebook beside a narrow bed.

Most famous promises fade within a generation. This one is a hundred and twenty-nine years old, and the lines are not getting shorter. Why won't it fade?

## 14:49 — THE LINE NOBODY QUOTES

Here it is. On that same day, in that same notebook, right after "I want to spend my heaven doing good on earth," Thérèse said this:

"I can't rest as long as there are souls to be saved."

She said she would rest only when the angel announced that time was over.

Read that again. Her promise does not have an end date. It has a condition. It lasts exactly as long as there is someone left on earth who needs to be brought home to God.

So the question is not really whether a dead nun is still keeping her word. The question is whether the condition is still true.

Look at your own family. Look at the empty chair at your table. The son who stopped going to Mass. The daughter who says she does not believe anymore. The grandchild who was never baptised.

She was promising to keep working for souls until there were none left to work for. A promise like that cannot be used up by one sad story, or by a century of them.
