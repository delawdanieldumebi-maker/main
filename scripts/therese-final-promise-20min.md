## 00:00 — Cold open

On the 10th of May, 2025, two days after he was elected, a new pope walked into the Basilica of St. Mary Major in Rome and stopped at a plain stone tomb. Pope Leo the Fourteenth was standing over his predecessor, Pope Francis, who had been buried there two weeks earlier. In his hand was one white rose. He set it down on the stone.

Nobody who knew Francis needed that explained. For decades he had kept a small habit that he told a pair of journalists about while he was still an archbishop in Buenos Aires. When a problem was too big for him, he would hand it to a young French nun who had been dead for a century. He did not ask her to fix it. He asked her to take it in her hands. And, he said, a white rose almost always came.

That nun was Thérèse of Lisieux. She died at twenty-four, in a convent infirmary in Normandy, in 1897. And one summer afternoon, a few weeks before she died, she made a promise that a pope's white rose was quietly pointing back to.

Most people know half of that promise. Tonight you are going to hear the other half, the half that explains why it has not stopped, and why it is still unfolding as you watch this.

## 01:44 — The promise and the question

The 17th of July, 1897. Thérèse has been in the infirmary for nine days. Her lungs are failing. Her older sister Pauline, known in the convent as Mother Agnes of Jesus, is sitting by the bed with a notebook, writing down what the sick nun says.

That day Thérèse told her that she felt her mission was about to begin. Then she said the sentence that would travel around the world: "I want to spend my heaven doing good on earth."

That is the half everyone quotes. But she did not stop there. A few lines later in the same notebook she added a condition. She said she could not rest as long as there were souls to be saved.

Read that again. The promise has no end date. It has a condition. And the condition decides how long it lasts.

So here is the question this whole story turns on. Why would a promise made by an unknown nun in 1897 still be unfolding right now, in our own year, and not merely remembered? Hold that question. The answer is hidden in a hospital in the city of Caen, and we will get there. First, you need to know who made the promise, and who she made it to.

## 03:03 — Who she was

She was born Thérèse Martin on the 2nd of January, 1873, in Alençon, the youngest of nine children. Four of them died young. When she was four, her mother, Zélie, died of breast cancer. The little girl who had been loud and laughing became fragile and tearful, and stayed that way for years.

Her father, Louis, moved his five daughters to Lisieux. One by one, the older girls entered the Carmelite convent there. Thérèse wanted to follow so badly that at fifteen she got permission, after appeals that went as far as the Pope himself. On the 9th of April, 1888, she went through the enclosure door. She never came out again.

What happened behind that door looked like nothing. She swept. She did laundry. She put up with sisters who got on her nerves. And out of those ordinary days she worked out what she would call her little way. She was not a great soul, she said, and she could not climb to God by heroic deeds. So she would let God carry her, the way a father carries a child up the stairs. Small acts done with love. That was the whole method.

Then, in April of 1896, on the night of Holy Thursday, she coughed up blood. It was tuberculosis. And within days something worse came. The sense of heaven that had carried her all her life went dark. For the last eighteen months of her life she said she lived in a night of faith, believing with her will while feeling nothing at all.

She made her promise from inside that night. Keep that in mind, because the people she made it to were about to need exactly that kind of faith.

## 04:57 — The two brothers

Here is a part of her story that is easy to skip, and it holds the key.

In October of 1895, a letter reached the Carmel from a young seminarian in Normandy named Maurice Bellière. He wanted to be a missionary. He knew he was weak. He asked the nuns to give him a sister who would pray for him every day for the rest of her life. The prioress chose Thérèse.

She had wanted a brother all her life. Her parents' two sons had died as babies. Now she had one, and she took the job seriously. She prayed for him. She wrote to him: patient, funny, tough when he needed it.

The next summer she was given a second one, a young priest of the Paris Foreign Missions named Adolphe Roulland, who was about to sail for China. Before he left, he came to the convent and spoke with her through the grille of the parlour. It was the only time she ever saw either of her brothers.

Now notice what she wrote to them as she was dying. To Father Roulland, in July of 1897, the same month as the notebook, she said plainly that she was counting on not staying idle in heaven. She meant to go on working for the Church and for souls. To Maurice she kept saying, in one form or another, that she would be nearer to him after her death than she could ever be from behind a convent wall.

So the famous promise was not first spoken to the world. It was spoken to two young men. That is the shape of it. Personal. One soul at a time.

She died on the 30th of September, 1897. Her last words were, "My God, I love you."

## 06:48 — How the promise left the convent

What came next is so well documented that it reads like a ledger.

A year after her death, her convent printed her memoirs as a small book, the way Carmels sent out notices when a sister died. It was called *Story of a Soul*. People who read it lent it to people who had never heard of Lisieux. It was reprinted again and again, then translated, and letters started arriving at the convent by the thousand from people who said they had prayed to her and that something had happened.

During the First World War, soldiers in the trenches carried her picture. In 1925 she was declared a saint. In 1927 this woman who never left her enclosure was named patron of the missions, beside Francis Xavier. And in 1997 she was named a Doctor of the Church, the highest title the Church gives a teacher.

So the promise worked. That much is on paper. But it also picked up something along the way, and it is worth being honest about.

## 07:52 — The myth to correct

You have probably seen the rose novena. Pray it for nine days, the card says, and if Thérèse hears you, she will send you a rose.

Here is the record. That novena did not come from Thérèse. It came from a Jesuit priest, Father Putigan, who started it in December of 1925, the year she was canonized, and asked for a rose as his own private sign. It is a lovely devotion and many people pray it with real faith. But Thérèse never promised anyone a rose on demand. The Church has never promised it either.

What she promised was larger and harder to measure: to spend her heaven doing good. The roses were her picture for that good. They were never a receipt.

Hold on to that difference. It changes everything about what you should look for when you ask her for help.

## 08:46 — The witnesses

So who has said she kept her word? Look at the kind of people who say so, because the list is surprising.

In 1928, a young woman from Skopje joined a missionary order and had to choose a name. She chose Teresa, after the nun of Lisieux, the patron of missionaries. The world would come to know her as Mother Teresa of Calcutta.

In 1960, Dorothy Day, the American journalist who had once been a socialist and an unbeliever, published a whole book about Thérèse. She had first found her dull. She said the little way won her over anyway, because it was a holiness a tired mother in a tenement could actually live.

In 2008, an American astronaut named Ron Garan flew on the space shuttle *Discovery*. A community of Carmelite nuns in Texas had given him a small relic of Thérèse, and he carried it into orbit. The girl who wanted to be a missionary on every continent at once went around the Earth more than two hundred times.

And then there was Jorge Bergoglio in Buenos Aires, with a vase of white roses in front of her picture in his office. He took that devotion to the papacy, and when he died, a white rose lay on his tomb.

A nun, a convert, an astronaut, a pope. None of them proves a miracle. But every one of them is a person who took a dead woman at her word and organised a part of their life around it. That is what the record can show: not that she acted, but that people kept acting on her promise.

## 11:56 — The hospital in Caen

But we still have not answered the question. Why does it not stop? To answer it, we have to go back to one of the two brothers. I told you the answer was hidden in a hospital in Caen.

Maurice Bellière did become a missionary. He was ordained, he went to Africa, and he worked there. Then his health broke. He came home to Normandy confused and very ill. On the 8th of June, 1907, he was admitted to the Bon Sauveur hospital in Caen.

The Bon Sauveur in Caen was the same hospital where Louis Martin, Thérèse's own father, had been cared for in the years his mind failed. Thérèse had prayed for her father behind convent walls while he lay in that place. Now her spiritual brother lay in the same place.

Maurice died there on the 14th of July, 1907. He was thirty-three. It was ten years, almost to the week, since she had promised from her infirmary to spend her heaven doing good.

Sit with that, because this is where people misread her promise.

If her promise meant that nothing bad would happen to the people she loved, then it failed the very first person she made it to. He did not get a long, easy life. He got Africa, illness and confusion, and a ward in the hospital where her father had suffered.

But that is not what she promised. She never promised to take the hospital away. She promised not to rest. To keep working. To stay close. And the one place on earth where her own father had suffered most was exactly the place her brother ended up, as if she had walked that ground before him so he would not walk it alone.

Nobody can prove that she was there. But it matches her words, and it matches the woman who wrote them.

## 13:52 — The answer

So here is the answer, and it is simpler than any prophecy.

Her promise is unfolding right now because the condition she attached to it is still true. She said she could not rest as long as there were souls to be saved. Look around your own kitchen table. Look at your own family. The condition has not run out. Not in 1907, not in 1925, not in 2026.

And it unfolds the way she first gave it, one person at a time. Not to crowds, but to a seminarian who felt too weak and a young priest heading for a country he had never seen. The crowds came later. The promise was always personal.

That is why it lasts. A promise to the world can be checked once and filed away. A promise to each person has to be kept again every time someone new asks.

## 16:00 — The little way, this week

So what do you do with her promise this week? Her way was always small. So start small.

Pick one name. Every evening this week, say one decade of the rosary for that person. Only one. Do not wait to feel anything. She did not.

Go to one weekday Mass and offer it for them without telling them.

If it has been years since your last confession, go. Tell the priest it has been a long time and you are not sure how to start. Priests hear that every week.

And there is probably one person in your house who is hard to love right now. Do one kind thing for them that they will never notice. That was the little way in its purest form: love that nobody sees, offered for someone who may never know.

None of that needs a relic or a rose. It needs exactly what Thérèse had in that infirmary: trust, and the next small thing.
