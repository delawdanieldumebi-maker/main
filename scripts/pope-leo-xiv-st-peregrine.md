# Why Pope Leo XIV's Cancer Sent the World to the Saint Who Was the Pope's Enemy

**Channel:** Christian Way · **Target length:** ~20 min (~3,100 words)

---

## 1. Cold open

It is the night of October 13th, at Gemelli Hospital in Rome. On the tenth floor, the Pope's apartment is dark. Only a small lamp is on beside the bed. A few hours earlier, surgeons removed a section of Pope Leo XIV's right lung. Down in the street, people are still standing behind the barriers, rosaries in their hands, looking up at a lit window.

Seven hundred years earlier, in a convent in northern Italy, another man spent his night before surgery in the dark. He was an old friar, and the next morning a doctor was coming to cut off his leg. He dragged himself to a crucifix, and according to the account written about him, what happened there changed his life a second time.

That friar is the saint Catholics around the world are calling on tonight for the Pope.

And yet, when he was young, this man was the Pope's enemy. He fought against papal rule, and he raised his hand against a man the Pope had sent.

So why, of all the saints in heaven, is the Church turning to him now? To answer that, we have to start with a Sunday in Rome.

## 2. The news

Sunday, October 11th. St. Peter's Square is full for the Angelus, the way it is every week. The Pope finishes the prayer, and instead of the usual greetings to the groups in the square, he says something no one expected.

He tells the crowd that during routine medical tests, doctors found a small nodule in his lung. He explains that he has decided to have it removed. He speaks calmly and briefly, like a father who does not want to frighten his children. Then he asks for one thing. He asks them to pray for him.

Within the hour, the news is on every screen in the world. The Vatican confirms that the Holy Father will be admitted to Gemelli at the start of the week, and that all his audiences are cancelled until October 20th.

On Tuesday morning, October 13th, the car pulls up at the hospital entrance. A crowd has been waiting since dawn. Leo steps out, raises his hand to them, and walks in on his own.

The operation is carried out that afternoon by the hospital's thoracic surgery team. That evening, the Holy See announces that the procedure has been completed as planned and that the Pope has returned to his room. Reports that night say the surgeons removed the lower lobe of the right lung, and that the tissue showed an early-stage adenocarcinoma, a form of lung cancer. The doctors speak of a favorable outlook, because it was caught early. But the word is out now, and the world reads it on every front page. The Pope has cancer.

Now look at the date. October 13th.

On that day in 1917, in a field in Portugal called the Cova da Iria, tens of thousands of people watched the sun move in the sky, just as Our Lady had told three shepherd children it would. Every year since then, October 13th has been the great day of Fatima. And this year, while the Pope was being taken into the operating room, the square at Fatima was full again. On the night of the 12th, by the shrine's count, well over a hundred thousand pilgrims held candles during the vigil and prayed for him. The next morning, at the international Mass, the celebrant ended his homily by turning toward the statue of the Virgin and placing the Pope in her care. The bishops of Portugal had already done the same two days before.

And it does not stop there. In Latin America, bishops ask the faithful to pray to Our Lady of Guadalupe for him. In Peru, in Chiclayo, the diocese where Robert Prevost served as bishop, people gather in front of his photograph without anyone organizing it. These are the families he visited and the poor he fed, and now they are praying for him. In the United States, the country where he was born, parishes add his name to the prayers at every Mass. Outside Gemelli, at all hours, small groups pray the rosary on the pavement.

But in the middle of all these prayers, one name keeps coming up that many Catholics had never heard before.

## 3. The name

When a Catholic hears the word cancer, there is one saint the Church has offered them for centuries. He is the patron of cancer patients, of the chronically ill, and of anyone facing an operation they are afraid of. His name is Peregrine Laziosi.

Since Sunday, his name has been in comment sections, prayer chains and parish bulletins all over the world. Many people are praying his novena for the Pope for the first time in their lives.

There is one detail that makes this even more striking. The most important shrine to St. Peregrine in the United States is not in a small town somewhere. It is in Chicago. It sits inside the Basilica of Our Lady of Sorrows on the city's West Side, where the Servite friars have prayed for the sick every week for generations. Chicago is the city where Robert Prevost was born.

So the Pope's own city has been praying, for decades, to the saint the whole world is now calling on for him.

But who was Peregrine? To understand him, we have to go back more than seven centuries, to a city that had declared war on the Pope.

## 4. A city against the Pope

We are in Forlì, in Romagna, in the north of Italy, at the end of the thirteenth century. Forlì belongs to the Papal States, which means the Pope is not only its spiritual father. He is also its ruler. And Forlì does not want him.

Italy at this time is torn between two parties. The Guelphs support the Pope. The Ghibellines support the Emperor. Forlì is Ghibelline, and proud of it. Its captain is a famous soldier named Guido da Montefeltro.

On May 1st, 1282, an army sent by the Pope arrives at the walls of Forlì to bring the city to heel. It is a large army, and many of its soldiers are French. Guido lets them into the city, then springs a trap. By evening, the streets are full of the dead. So many fall that, a few years later, the poet Dante will remember Forlì in the Divine Comedy as the city that made "a bloody heap" of the French.

For the people of Forlì, it is a great victory. For the Pope, it is an open rebellion. And the Church answers with the heaviest spiritual punishment it has. The city is placed under interdict.

It is hard for us today to imagine what that means. Under interdict, the churches of Forlì are closed. No Mass is celebrated. The bells are silent. Couples cannot marry in church, and the dead cannot be buried with the Church's rites. A whole city lives as if God had turned away from it.

In that city lives a young man from one of the wealthy families. He is about eighteen. His name is Peregrine. His family is on the side of the rebels, and so is he, with all the passion of his age. As far as he is concerned, the Pope is the enemy.

## 5. The envoy

Now the Pope wants peace, not more blood. So he sends a man to Forlì who is not a soldier, a judge or a tax collector. He sends a friar.

His name is Philip Benizi, and he is one of the most respected men in the Church. He was born in Florence, he studied medicine in Paris and Padua, and he practiced as a doctor. Then he left everything to join a young religious order called the Servants of Mary, the Servites. They are friars devoted to the sorrows of the Virgin at the foot of the cross. By now, Philip leads the entire order as its Prior General.

There is a story about him that tells you who he was. In 1268, the cardinals spent almost three years unable to agree on a new pope, and some of them began to say Philip's name. When he heard about it, Philip did not wait to find out. He left the city and hid in the mountains until the danger had passed, because he did not want to be pope.

That is the man who arrives in Forlì to preach peace.

He stands in the square and talks about reconciliation. He calls the city to return to unity with the Church. He asks them to open their hearts again so that the churches can open their doors again.

The crowd does not want to hear it. To them, he is the Pope's man. They shout him down and jeer at him. They push him toward the city gate.

And according to the tradition passed down in Forlì and in the Servite order, among the most violent in that crowd was the young Peregrine. He did not just shout. He went up to Philip Benizi and struck him across the face.

## 6. The other cheek

Think about that moment. A young man from a good family, full of anger and certain he is right, hits a friar in front of the whole city. The crowd is watching. Everyone expects something to follow, whether anger, a curse or a threat.

Philip does none of those things. According to the tradition, he does not raise his hand or his voice. He looks at the young man, turns his face, and offers him the other cheek, just as the Gospel says. Then he leaves the city, praying for the one who struck him.

Peregrine stays in the square. The crowd moves on, but something has broken open in him. He had come ready for a fight, and what he got was the Gospel lived in front of him, by a man who had every reason to hate him.

The tradition says that Peregrine could not bear it. He left the crowd and ran out of the city after the friar. He caught up with him on the road outside Forlì, fell on his knees in the dust, and asked his forgiveness.

Philip forgave him. And for Peregrine, nothing would ever be the same.

## 7. A new road

After that day, the young man starts spending long hours in prayer. He no longer recognizes the life he had been living. One day, in the cathedral of Forlì, according to the account handed down by his order, the Virgin Mary appears to him. She tells him to go to Siena and join the friars there, the ones they call her servants.

The order she sent him to was the order of the man he had struck.

Peregrine obeys. He leaves his family, his money and his name in Forlì, and walks to Siena. There he asks to join the Servants of Mary. Tradition says it was Philip Benizi himself who received him into the order. The friar who had been struck opened the door to the one who had struck him.

There is one detail here that is easy to miss. The Servites, like many orders of that time, live by the Rule of St. Augustine. Seven centuries later, a young man from Chicago named Robert Prevost will join the Order of St. Augustine. He will live by that same rule, and one day he will become Pope Leo XIV.

So the saint people are praying to for the Pope was formed by the same rule of life that formed the Pope himself.

## 8. Standing for others

After his years of formation, Peregrine is sent home. He goes back to Forlì, the city that had cast out the Pope's envoy, as a priest of the order of that envoy. He helps to found a Servite house there, and he spends the rest of his life serving the people he once marched with.

He becomes the friar everyone in Forlì looks for. He hears confessions for hours. He preaches. He goes to the poor and to the sick, and he is known for his patience with sinners, because he had been one himself.

And he takes on an unusual penance. To make up for his past, he resolves never to sit down unless he truly has to. He prays standing, he works standing and he hears confessions standing. Some accounts say he kept this up for thirty years.

At first it seems like a small thing. But the body keeps count. Year after year, the veins in his legs weaken. By the time Peregrine is around sixty, one leg has become swollen and dark, and a wound opens on it.

## 9. The wound

The wound does not close. Each month it spreads further, and it starts to smell so strongly that people stay back when he passes. The accounts of his life describe it as a cancer eating into his leg. Today we cannot know for certain what doctors would call it. What we do know is that it was eating away his flesh, slowly, and that in those days there was only one cure.

The convent's doctor, Paolo Salazio, examines the leg and gives his verdict. It has to come off, or the disease will kill him. The operation is set for the next morning.

Imagine what that meant in the fourteenth century. There was no anesthesia, no sterile room and no antibiotics. All the doctor had was a saw, a fire to seal the wound, and friars to hold the patient down. Many did not survive it.

That evening, Peregrine does what he has done every day for decades. He goes to pray. He cannot really walk anymore, so he drags himself along the corridor, one step at a time, to the chapter room, where a large crucifix hangs on the wall.

He spends the night there.

According to the accounts, he asks to be healed. But he does not demand it. He does not bargain with God. He places his leg, his fear and his life in God's hands, ready to accept the knife in the morning if that is what the Lord wants.

Then, worn out, he falls asleep at the foot of the cross.

And in that sleep, the accounts tell, he sees Christ come down from the cross. The Lord bends over the old friar and lays His hand on the diseased leg.

In the morning, the doctor arrives with his instruments. He uncovers the leg to begin, and stops. The wound is closed. The skin is whole. According to the accounts, there is not even a scar where the disease had been. Paolo Salazio, the man of science who had seen the wound the day before, tells everyone what he has seen.

The news goes through Forlì in a day. The city that had once lived under interdict now had a miracle in its own convent.

## 10. Three hundred years

Peregrine lived about twenty more years. He did not use them to become famous. He went back to his confessional, to his poor and to his sick, and he went on standing.

He died on May 1st, 1345, at about the age of eighty. Some say he was already older.

Remember that date, May 1st. It is the same day of the year on which, sixty-three years earlier, Forlì had filled its streets with the dead in its war against the Pope. The day the city remembered for its rebellion became the day it would remember for its saint.

The people of Forlì came in such crowds to his funeral that the friars could not bury him for days. His body is still kept today in the basilica that bears his name in the center of Forlì, in a glass urn beneath the altar, where pilgrims come to pray.

The Church moved slowly, as it always does. Peregrine was beatified in 1609. Then, on December 27th, 1726, Pope Benedict XIII declared him a saint.

That means that this December, in about ten weeks, it will be exactly three hundred years since the rebel of Forlì was raised to the altars.

From Italy, devotion to him spread wherever the Servite friars went. They reached Spain, Austria and, eventually, America. In Chicago, in the twentieth century, the Servites at Our Lady of Sorrows began weekly prayers that drew tens of thousands of people. Over the years, St. Peregrine became the friend of the cancer ward, of the chemotherapy room and of the family sitting in a hospital corridor at night.

And Philip Benizi, the man who was struck? He died in 1285, only a few years after the slap, and he too was declared a saint, in 1671. They are two saints from the same order. One struck the other, and both are now in heaven.

## 11. Why him

So why Peregrine? Why does the Church, when its Pope is sick, turn to the saint who was once the Pope's enemy? There are three reasons, and each one says something about us.

The first is forgiveness. Peregrine is only a saint because someone forgave him first. If Philip Benizi had hit back, cursed him or reported him, there would be no St. Peregrine. There would just be one more angry young man in a rebel city. Philip's forgiveness lasted longer than Philip did. The friar died in 1285, but the man he forgave went on to heal and console people for sixty years. And seven hundred years later, that same forgiveness is praying for a pope. We never know how far one act of mercy will reach. Philip did not live to see Peregrine become a saint. He only had to forgive.

The second is surrender. Look again at the night before the surgery. Peregrine did not demand a miracle. He did not say, "Lord, if You love me, You will heal me." He said, in effect, "Lord, I am Yours, whatever happens tomorrow." The miracle came afterward, and as a gift. On that Sunday in St. Peter's Square, Pope Leo did not ask the world for a miracle either. He asked for prayers, and he entrusted himself to them. That is how a Christian faces an operating room. We do not try to force God's hand. We put ourselves in it.

The third is family. Think about who is praying tonight. There are pilgrims in Portugal and the poor of Chiclayo. There are parishioners in Chicago, in the same city and the same church where St. Peregrine has been prayed to for generations. And there is a saint from Forlì who once raised his hand against the Pope and now raises it for him. That is the Church, a family where old enemies become brothers, where the strong carry the weak, and where no one faces the night before surgery alone. Even the Pope needs his family to pray for him.

## 12. Prayer and call to action

Maybe tonight you are not thinking only of the Pope. Maybe you are thinking of your mother waiting for test results, or of a husband, a child or a friend in a chemotherapy chair. Maybe you are the one with a scan scheduled, afraid of what the doctor will say.

St. Peregrine knows that fear. He spent a whole night with it at the foot of a cross. So let us pray to him together, now, wherever you are.

*St. Peregrine, you who were forgiven and learned to forgive,*
*you who kept watch through the night before your surgery,*
*stay beside Pope Leo in his recovery.*
*Ask the Lord to give him strength, healing and many years to serve His Church.*
*Watch over everyone who is fighting cancer tonight,*
*over the doctors who care for them,*
*and over the families who wait and hope.*
*And teach us, like you, to place our lives in the hands of the Crucified.*
*Our Lady of Sorrows, Our Lady of Fatima, protect the Holy Father.*
*Amen.*
