# Why Pope Leo XIV's Cancer Sent the World to the Saint Who Was the Pope's Enemy

**Channel:** Christian Way · **Target length:** ~25 min · **Narration:** "we" voice, calm and reverent

---

## 1. Cold Open

It is the night before surgery.

In a quiet room on the tenth floor of the Gemelli hospital in Rome, the Bishop of Rome, the Successor of Peter, the shepherd of more than a billion Catholics, is waiting for the morning. In a few hours, a team of thoracic surgeons will open his chest and remove a piece of his right lung.

Seven hundred years earlier, in a small convent in the north of Italy, another man in another habit spent another night just like this one. He too was waiting for the surgeons. He too knew that in the morning, part of his body would be cut away.

He did not sleep in a bed. He dragged himself across a stone floor to the foot of a crucifix. And what happened there, according to the account handed down by his brothers, would make him the saint that the Church has entrusted with the sick for three centuries.

Today, Catholics all over the world are calling on that saint for Pope Leo the Fourteenth.

And yet there is something about this man that almost no one mentions. In his youth, he was not a friend of the papacy. He was its enemy. He stood in a crowd that drove the Pope's own envoy out of his city, and he raised his hand and struck that envoy across the face.

So why is the Church turning to him now? Why this saint, for this Pope, on this day?

To answer that, we will have to go back to a rebel city, to a slap that should have been forgotten, and to a doctor who refused to become Pope. But first, let us begin where the whole world began this week.

---

## 2. The Week the World Held Its Breath

It was Sunday, October 11th. St. Peter's Square was full, as it always is for the Angelus. The pilgrims had come to pray with the Pope, to hear a few words, to receive a blessing and go home.

But at the end of the prayer, Leo the Fourteenth said something no one in the square had expected. During routine medical checks, he explained, doctors had found a small nodule in his lung. He had decided to have it removed. And he asked, simply, for the prayers of the faithful.

There was no drama in his voice. No fear. Just a man telling his family he was going into the hospital.

Within hours, the news had crossed every ocean. The Vatican canceled the Pope's audiences through October 20th.

On Tuesday, October 13th, Leo arrived at the Gemelli, the same hospital where, forty-five years earlier, surgeons had fought for the life of John Paul the Second after the bullets of St. Peter's Square.

The operation was performed by the thoracic surgery team led by Professor Stefano Margaritora. That afternoon, the Holy See press office released its bulletin. The procedure had been completed successfully, within the expected time and in the planned manner. There had been no complications. The Holy Father had returned to his room.

And then came the word that everyone had been afraid of. The tests confirmed what the doctors had suspected before surgery. It was lung adenocarcinoma. To remove the disease completely, the surgeons had taken out the lower lobe of his right lung.

It is important to say this clearly, because fear travels faster than facts. Adenocarcinoma is the most common form of lung cancer, and caught early, it carries the most hopeful outlook. Even before the operation, oncologists had called the Pope's prognosis excellent, precisely because the nodule was found so early. Further tests are still underway.

But for millions of Catholics, one detail was impossible to ignore. The date.

October 13th.

On October 13th, 1917, at the Cova da Iria in Portugal, tens of thousands watched the sun tremble in the sky, exactly as Our Lady had announced to three shepherd children. And at Fatima, she had asked those children again and again to pray for the Holy Father.

So on the very day the Pope lay on the operating table, the pilgrims gathered at Fatima for the anniversary did exactly that. In Chiclayo, Peru, where Robert Prevost had been bishop, people prayed for the man they still call one of their own. In Rome, outside the Gemelli, people stood with rosaries, looking up at the windows.

And in the middle of all these prayers, one name began to appear over and over. A name many Catholics had never heard before.

Saint Peregrine.

When a Catholic is told the word "cancer," there is one saint the Church has entrusted them to for centuries. Peregrine Laziosi, a friar of the Servants of Mary, is the patron saint of those who suffer from cancer.

And there is a detail that moved many people when they discovered it. The most important shrine to Saint Peregrine in all of the United States, the National Shrine of St. Peregrine, is found inside Our Lady of Sorrows Basilica in Chicago. Chicago, the city where Robert Francis Prevost was born in 1955. For generations, families there have lit candles before Saint Peregrine for a mother, a father, a child with cancer. This week, they lit one for the boy from their city who became Pope.

It is a beautiful coincidence. But it is not the most surprising part of this story.

Because if you go back far enough, the saint who is now praying for the Pope was once standing on the other side.

---

## 3. A City Without Sacraments

We are in Forlì, in the region of Romagna, in the north of Italy, at the end of the thirteenth century.

Forlì is a proud and wealthy city. It belongs, on paper, to the Papal States. But in practice, it does not want to obey the Pope. Italy at this time is torn between two great factions. On one side, the Guelphs, who support the papacy. On the other, the Ghibellines, who support the emperor. And Forlì is a Ghibelline city, defiant and armed.

The conflict is not only about politics. It is about power, taxes, armies, and pride. And in 1282, the Pope decides to answer the rebellion with the strongest weapon the Church has. He places Forlì under interdict.

It is difficult for us today to imagine what this meant. Under an interdict, the churches of the city are closed to public worship. No public Mass is celebrated. The bells fall silent. The ordinary rhythm of Christian life, the Sunday Mass, the feast days, the processions, simply stops. A whole city is cut off from the life of the Church as a sign that it has cut itself off from the Church's shepherd.

In one of the wealthy families of Forlì, the Laziosi family, there is a young man named Peregrine. He was born around 1265. His name, in Latin, means "the pilgrim," the one who travels far from home. No one in his family could know how fitting that name would one day become.

Peregrine is not a quiet, pious boy. The accounts describe him as a young man of the city, quick-tempered and passionate, completely caught up in the cause of his faction. His family stands with the rebels. And he, still very young, is among the most fervent of them all.

For Peregrine, the Pope is not a spiritual father. He is a political adversary. A distant power trying to take away his city's freedom.

And then, one day, the Pope sends someone to Forlì.

---

## 4. The Man Who Refused to Be Pope

To understand what happens next, we need to meet the man the Pope sends. Because he is not an ordinary messenger.

His name is Philip Benizi. He was born in Florence in 1233, into a noble family. As a young man, he studied medicine at the great universities of Paris and Padua and became a doctor. Remember that detail. This story begins with a doctor and it will end with one.

But Philip did not stay in medicine. According to the tradition of his order, while he was praying one day, he received a call to join a young religious community in Florence, the Servants of Mary. They were a new order, founded by seven Florentine merchants who had left their wealth behind to live in poverty, prayer, and devotion to the sorrows of the Virgin Mary.

And here is the first surprising thing about Philip Benizi. He entered the order as a simple lay brother. He hid his education. The doctor from Paris and Padua asked to work in the garden and beg for alms. It was only when his superiors discovered who he was that they ordered him to be ordained a priest.

From there, his life moved quickly. In 1267, he was elected Prior General of the whole order. He traveled across Italy and Europe, preaching, reconciling, making peace between cities at war.

And then came the second surprising thing. After the death of Pope Clement the Fourth in 1268, the cardinals gathered to elect his successor. The conclave dragged on for almost three years, the longest in the history of the Church. And according to the tradition of the Servite order, during those years, Philip Benizi's name was mentioned as a possible Pope.

When Philip heard this, he did not campaign. He did not wait. He ran. Tradition says he fled into the mountains and hid there until the danger had passed and another man had been chosen.

Think about that for a moment. This is the man who will walk into a rebel city as the Pope's envoy. A man who could have worn the white cassock himself and chose instead to hide in a cave.

So when the Holy See looks for someone to bring peace to Forlì, it sends a man who wants nothing for himself. No power, no title, no victory. Only peace.

And the city of Forlì is about to show him what it thinks of peace.

---

## 5. The Slap

It is around the year 1283. Philip Benizi enters Forlì. He does what he has done in so many other cities. He goes into the streets and the squares, and he preaches. He speaks of reconciliation. He calls the people to return to the unity of the Church.

But Forlì does not want to hear it.

A crowd forms around him. The shouting begins. Insults. Mockery. Someone pushes him. Then the crowd decides that the Pope's man has said enough, and they begin to drive him out of the city by force. According to the accounts, Philip is beaten as he is pushed through the streets toward the gates.

And in that crowd, among the loudest and angriest, is the young Peregrine Laziosi.

According to tradition, Peregrine pushes his way to the front. He stands face to face with the Pope's envoy, this priest, this physician, this man who had refused to become Pope. And he raises his hand and strikes him across the face.

In any other story, a blow would be answered with a blow.

But Philip Benizi does not strike back. He does exactly what the Gospel says, words every one of us has heard a hundred times and almost none of us has truly lived. "If anyone strikes you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also."

According to the accounts, Philip turns his face, and he prays for the young man who has just struck him.

We do not know exactly what Peregrine saw in that moment. Perhaps it was the calm in Philip's eyes. Perhaps it was the silence. Perhaps it was simply the shock of a strength he had never seen before. A strength that did not need to win.

But something breaks open inside him.

The crowd disperses. Philip leaves the city. And Peregrine cannot stop thinking about that face. Tradition says that he runs out after him, leaves the walls of Forlì behind, and catches up with Philip on the road. And there, the young rebel who had struck the Pope's envoy falls to his knees in the dust and begs for his forgiveness.

And Philip forgives him.

He does not make him earn it. He does not ask for an explanation. He lifts him up and embraces him.

We often think of forgiveness as the end of a story. Here, it is the beginning. Everything Peregrine will become flows from that one moment on a road outside Forlì, when a man who had been struck chose to bless instead.

---

## 6. The Pilgrim Sets Out

Peregrine returns home, but he is no longer the same man. The fire in him has not gone out. It has changed direction.

He begins to spend long hours in prayer. And according to the tradition of the Servite order, one day, while he is praying, the Virgin Mary appears to him. She tells him to go to Siena, where he will find the men who call themselves her servants, and to join them.

The order she sends him to is the order of Philip Benizi. The very man he had struck.

And so Peregrine, whose name means "the pilgrim," finally becomes one. He leaves his family, his wealth, his city, and the faction he had fought for, and he walks to Siena. There, he knocks on the door of the Servants of Mary and asks to be received.

He receives the habit and begins his life as a friar. And there is a detail here that, this week, has taken on a special meaning. The Servants of Mary, like several of the great religious families of that time, followed the Rule of Saint Augustine. The Rule written by the great bishop of Hippo, the Rule that shapes a life of community, prayer, and charity.

Seven hundred years later, the man who sits on the throne of Peter is himself a son of Saint Augustine. Robert Prevost is an Augustinian friar. He lived under the same ancient Rule that Peregrine embraced in Siena. Two friars, separated by seven centuries, formed by the same words of the same saint.

Peregrine is eventually ordained a priest. And after some years, his superiors make a decision that must have seemed strange to him. They send him back home. Back to Forlì.

---

## 7. The Friar Who Would Not Sit Down

Imagine what it must have meant for Peregrine to walk back through the gates of Forlì. The same streets where he had shouted against the Pope. The same squares where he had struck a priest. Now he walks through them wearing the black habit of the Servants of Mary, the order of the man he had attacked.

And the people of Forlì slowly discover that the angry young rebel has become someone else entirely.

He preaches. He hears confessions for hours. He becomes known as a man who can reconcile enemies, perhaps because he knows better than anyone what it means to be one. He spends his days with the poor and especially with the sick, visiting them, praying with them, caring for them.

And he takes on a penance that people in Forlì would remember for generations. According to tradition, Peregrine resolved never to sit down unless it was absolutely necessary. When he prayed, he stood. When he ate, he stood. When he was tired, he stood. Some accounts say he kept this penance for some thirty years.

He also embraced silence and solitude whenever he could. Not as a punishment, but as a way of making room for God. A man who had once used his strength against the Church now used it to keep watch for her.

The years pass. The young man of the faction grows old in the service of the people he once led into the streets. And his body begins to pay the price.

---

## 8. The Night Before Surgery

Peregrine is around sixty years old when a sore appears on his leg.

At first it seems small. But it does not heal. The veins of his legs, after so many years of standing, have become diseased. The wound grows. It spreads. It becomes open and painful, and according to the accounts, it gives off a smell so terrible that people find it difficult to stay near him.

The chroniclers of the time call it a cancer. With our modern knowledge, we cannot say with certainty exactly what disease Peregrine had. But we know what the people around him saw. A wound that was eating away at his body and that no remedy could stop.

For a man who had spent his life visiting the sick, it was now his turn to become one of them.

The convent called a physician. The accounts give us his name, Paolo Salazio. He examined the leg and came to the only conclusion that medicine could offer in the fourteenth century. The infection was spreading. If the leg was not removed, the friar would die. The amputation was set for the next morning.

In the fourteenth century, there was no anesthesia. No antibiotics. An amputation was a terrible ordeal, and many did not survive it.

Peregrine knew this. And on the night before the operation, he did not stay in his bed.

According to the accounts handed down by his brothers, he dragged himself, with that diseased leg, to the chapter room of the convent, where a large crucifix hung on the wall. And there, at the foot of the cross, he began to pray.

He prayed for a long time. And here is the most important detail of this whole story, the detail that is so easy to miss. The accounts do not describe a man demanding a miracle. They describe a man surrendering. He asked to be healed, yes. But he placed himself completely in God's hands, ready to accept the knife, the pain, even death, if that was what the Lord wanted for him.

Exhausted, he fell asleep there, at the foot of the crucifix.

And in his sleep, according to the account, he saw Christ come down from the cross. Jesus bent over him, reached out, and touched his diseased leg.

The next morning, Paolo Salazio arrived to perform the amputation. He uncovered the leg.

And according to the tradition of the Servants of Mary, the wound was gone.

The flesh had closed. The leg was healthy. The physician who had come to cut found nothing left to cut. The friars ran through the convent. The news spread through Forlì. The man who had struck the Pope's envoy, the man who had stood for thirty years, the man who had prayed all night at the foot of the cross, was walking.

---

## 9. Eighty Years and Three Centuries

Now, some will say that this is a legend. That medieval chroniclers loved miracles and did not always distinguish between what happened and what was hoped for.

It is a fair question, and the Church does not ask anyone to read an ancient account as a medical report.

But consider what we do know. Peregrine did not die of that disease. He lived about twenty more years, still preaching, still caring for the sick. And the people of Forlì, who had seen the wound and then saw him walking, never forgot it.

Peregrine Laziosi died on May 1st, 1345, at about eighty years of age. Almost immediately, the people of Forlì began to venerate him as a saint. Sick people came to his tomb. Graces were reported. The devotion spread beyond Forlì, beyond Romagna, beyond Italy.

Today his body rests in the basilica that bears his name in the heart of Forlì, where pilgrims still come to pray before it.

After a long examination of his life and the graces attributed to him, Pope Benedict the Thirteenth declared him a saint on December 27th, 1726.

Which means that this December, just over two months from now, the Church will mark three hundred years since the canonization of Saint Peregrine.

And Philip Benizi, the man he struck? He died in 1285 in the city of Todi, not long after the events in Forlì, in the order he had served so faithfully. He too was declared a saint, by Pope Clement the Tenth in 1671.

Two men. One raised his hand. The other turned his cheek. Today, both of them are on the altars of the Church.

---

## 10. Why This Saint, for This Pope

So now we can return to our question. Why is the Church turning to Saint Peregrine today? Not just because he is the patron of the sick. There are three threads in his story that seem to have been woven for exactly this moment.

**The first thread is forgiveness.**

Look again at what happened. A young man rose up against the Pope. He struck the Pope's envoy. Seven hundred years later, that same young man is the saint the faithful are asking to pray for a Pope.

This is what forgiveness does. It does not just close an old wound. It changes the future. Philip Benizi could not have known that the boy kneeling before him would one day be invoked in hospital rooms from Chicago to Rome. He simply forgave. And God took that one act of mercy and let it grow.

Forgiveness outlives the one who forgives. That is the first thing Saint Peregrine teaches us.

**The second thread is surrender.**

On the night before his surgery, Peregrine did not bargain with God. He did not say, "Heal me, or I will stop believing." He said, in effect, "Here I am. Do with me what You will."

And when Leo the Fourteenth stood at the window on Sunday, he did not make dramatic declarations. He did not hide the news. He told his family the truth, and he asked for their prayers.

There is a deep wisdom in this. Praying for someone who is sick is not a way of forcing God's hand. It is a way of placing that person, together, into the hands of the Father. When we pray for the Pope this week, we are not ordering heaven to obey us. We are carrying a brother's weight with him, as one family, so that he does not carry it alone.

**The third thread is the healing hand.**

This story is full of physicians. Philip Benizi was a doctor before he was a priest. Paolo Salazio was the doctor who came to amputate. And this week, a team of surgeons in Rome placed their skill at the service of the Pope's life.

The Church has never set faith against medicine. Saint Peregrine is not invoked instead of doctors, but alongside them. We ask the same Christ who, according to the account, touched a friar's leg in the night to guide the hands of every surgeon and nurse who cares for the sick. Grace does not replace the healer. It blesses him.

From Fatima to Peru, from Chicago to a hospital corridor in Rome: a whole family, spread across the world, gathered around one bed.

---

## 11. The Wound No One Sees

Perhaps, as you listen to this story, you are thinking of someone.

Perhaps someone you love has heard that word, "cancer," this year. Perhaps it was you. Perhaps you know what it is like to sit in a waiting room, to count the days until the next scan, to lie awake on the night before surgery, just as Peregrine did, just as the Pope did.

If that is you, Saint Peregrine understands. He knew the fear. He knew the pain. He knew what it was to wait for the morning and not know what it would bring.

Or perhaps the wound you carry is a different kind. Perhaps someone struck you, not on the face, but in the heart, and you have never been able to forgive. Then remember Philip Benizi on the streets of Forlì. Remember that one act of forgiveness, seven hundred years ago, is still changing lives today. Yours could too.

And perhaps, like the young Peregrine, you are the one who once raised your hand. You have hurt someone, or turned your back on God, and you think it is too late. Then remember the road outside Forlì. Remember a young man running after the one he had wounded, falling to his knees, and being lifted up. It is never too late to run after mercy.

## 12. Prayer and Call to Action

So let us pray together now, wherever you are listening. For the Holy Father, for all who suffer from cancer, and for their families.

you who once raised your hand in anger
and were lifted up by mercy,

and the long night before the surgeon came,
