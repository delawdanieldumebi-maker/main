#### Where the word comes from

*Synod* is Greek. *Syn* means "together." *Hodos* means "way" or "road." A synod is a coming-together on the road — which is where the popular phrase "walking together" comes from.

> 🎬 **SHOT 14** · ⏱ 4:44  
> **Visual:** Ancient road / Roman paving stones  
> **On screen:** Greek: σύν + ὁδός = "together" + "way"

But in the Church's actual history, the word had a very specific meaning. From the early centuries, *synod* in Greek and *council* in Latin were used almost interchangeably. Both meant the same thing: bishops gathered to settle questions of faith and discipline. The great councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon were called synods. In the Eastern Churches today, the governing body is called the Holy Synod — and it is made of bishops.

> 🎬 **SHOT 15** · ⏱ 4:57  
> **Visual:** Icon of the First Council of Nicaea  
> **On screen:** "SYNOD = COUNCIL"
>
> 🎬 **SHOT 16** · ⏱ 4:57

So for most of Christian history, the word had a clear answer to the question, "who walks together, and to do what?" Bishops, gathered with Peter, to decide. Keep that in mind. It matters later.

#### How the Council handled its own new words

And here's a detail that makes the absence even more telling. The Council was not careless with new language. When it did introduce a major new emphasis, it went out of its way to define it.

> 🎞️ **B-ROLL** · ⏱ 8:48  
> **Visual:** Archival Council session, wide  
> **On screen:** —

The clearest example is *collegiality* — the teaching that the bishops form a college with the Pope at its head. It was one of the most fiercely debated subjects of the entire Council. Some fathers feared it would be read as weakening the authority of the Pope. So in November 1964, before *Lumen Gentium* was approved, a *Preliminary Note of Explanation* was attached to the text, spelling out exactly what collegiality meant and what it did not mean — including that the college never acts without its head, and that the Pope remains free to exercise his authority on his own.

> 🎬 **SHOT 23** · ⏱ 9:03  
> **Visual:** Archival Council session, voting  
> **On screen:** Document card: *Nota Explicativa Praevia*

Sit with that for a moment. When the Council fathers introduced a new concept that touched the structure of the Church, they refused to leave it vague. They defined it, fenced it, and attached the definition to the document itself.

#### The Council of Jerusalem

Defenders of synodality often reach further back than Vatican II — all the way to the Acts of the Apostles, chapter 15. It's the first council in the Church's history, and it's worth reading closely, because it's the model both sides claim.

#### The German Synodal Way

In 2019, the bishops of Germany, together with the country's leading lay Catholic organization, launched what they called the *Synodaler Weg* — the Synodal Way. Its assemblies were made up of bishops and laypeople voting together. Over the next four years, those assemblies approved texts calling for blessings of same-sex couples, for re-examining the Church's teaching on sexuality, for studying the ordination of women, and for a permanent synodal council of bishops and laity to govern the Church in Germany going forward. **[VERIFY: resolutions as summarised]**

#### Second objection — and this one comes from a saint

In 1859, John Henry Newman wrote an essay called *On Consulting the Faithful in Matters of Doctrine*. In it, he argued that during the Arian crisis of the fourth century, when many bishops wavered on the divinity of Christ, it was in large part the ordinary faithful who held fast to the faith of Nicaea. Saint Jerome described that era by saying the whole world groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian.

#### Third objection: "Words develop."

*Homoousios* isn't in the Gospels. *Transubstantiation* isn't in Scripture. Why hold synodality to a different standard?

> 🎞️ **B-ROLL** · ⏱ 22:34  
> **Visual:** Text card  
> **On screen:** HOMOOUSIOS · TRANSUBSTANTIATION

Because those words were adopted to make the faith more precise. They were argued over, defined, fenced against error, and fixed by councils. They closed questions. Synodality has gone the other way: it arrived after the Council, has never been defined by one, and is used to reopen what was settled. A new word that adds precision is development. A new word that removes it is something else.

> 🎞️ **B-ROLL** · ⏱ 22:41  
> **Visual:** Painting of the Council of Trent  
> **On screen:** DEVELOPMENT = MORE PRECISION

And this standard isn't ours. It's ancient. In the year 434, Saint Vincent of Lérins wrote a short book called the *Commonitorium*, asking how a Christian can tell true teaching from error. His answer became famous: hold to what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all.

> 🎬 **SHOT 47** · ⏱ 23:09  
> **Visual:** Lérins island monastery (aerial)  
> **On screen:** Quote card: "everywhere, always, and by all"

But Vincent didn't deny that doctrine develops. He compared it to a child growing into a man — the same person, larger, stronger, but never turning into something else. Growth, he said, must happen within its own kind: in the same doctrine, the same sense, the same meaning. The First Vatican Council quoted those exact words in 1870 and made them part of its teaching. **[VERIFY: Dei Filius ch. 4 citation]**

> 🎬 **SHOT 48** · ⏱ 23:28  
> **Visual:** Acorn → oak time-lapse  
> **On screen:** Three phrases stack

The same sense. The same meaning. If a new word can be used to mean the opposite of what the Church taught before, it fails Vincent's test before you even get to the details.

> 🎞️ **B-ROLL** · ⏱ 23:55  
> **Visual:** Hold acorn → oak  
> **On screen:** Document card: *Dei Filius* ch. 4

Newman himself, the same Newman the other side quotes, wrote a whole book on this in 1845: *An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine*. He gave seven marks of a true development. Two of them settle our question. The first he called preservation of type — a true development keeps the same essential shape, the way an oak keeps the nature of the acorn. Another he called conservative action upon its past — a true development protects and confirms what came before it; it doesn't undo it.

> 🎬 **SHOT 49** · ⏱ 24:09  
> **Visual:** 1845 title page  
> **On screen:** Card: "Preservation of type" · "Conservative action upon its past"

Hold synodality up to those two marks. Does it preserve the type of a synod — bishops gathered with Peter? Its membership has already changed. Does it confirm what came before? In Germany, it was used to challenge teaching the Church had held for centuries, until Rome stepped in. By Newman's own criteria, those are warning signs, not marks of growth.

#### Back to 1965

On the morning of September 14th, 1965, Paul VI opened the fourth and final session of the Council in Saint Peter's Basilica. Before the assembled bishops, he announced a new body: a Synod of Bishops, drawn from around the world, to assist the Pope in his care for the universal Church.

> 🎬 **SHOT 54** · ⏱ 29:32  
> **Visual:** Archival footage of fourth-session opening  
> **On screen:** "14 SEPT 1965"

The next day he made it law, in the motu proprio *Apostolica Sollicitudo*. Read it, and you'll find its purposes laid out plainly. The synod was to foster close union between the Pope and the bishops of the world. It was to give the Pope direct, accurate information about the state of the Church in different places. And it was to help the bishops reach agreement on essential points of doctrine and on how the Church should act. By its nature, the document says, its task is to inform and to advise — and it can decide only when the Pope grants it that power. **[VERIFY: summary of Apostolica Sollicitudo, sections I–II]**
