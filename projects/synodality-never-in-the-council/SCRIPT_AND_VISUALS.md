## 2. Script

### PART 1

Sixteen documents. Four constitutions, nine decrees, three declarations. More than six hundred numbered articles, written over four sessions by more than two thousand bishops between 1962 and 1965. That's the entire written legacy of the Second Vatican Council. And there's one word you won't find anywhere in it.

The word is *synodality*.

Today it's the word used to describe what the Church is supposed to become. Pope Francis called it the path God expects of the Church in the third millennium. A worldwide synod was convened to study it. Dioceses on every continent held listening sessions in its name. And three senior churchmen — Bishop Athanasius Schneider, Cardinal Raymond Burke, and Cardinal Gerhard Müller — have each, from different directions, pointed at the gap in the Council's texts and asked a simple question: if this is the fruit of the Council, why didn't the Council ever say it?

Here's where this channel stands. We think they're right to ask, and we think the question has gone unanswered for too long. But where we stand doesn't prove anything. So today we're going to build the case only from sources you can open yourself: the texts of Vatican II, the Acts of the Apostles, the Code of Canon Law, a 2018 study by the International Theological Commission, the speeches of Pope Francis, and the 1965 decree that created the Synod of Bishops. Where the documents settle a point, I'll say so. Where we're making an argument, I'll call it an argument. And before we're done, we'll give the other side its strongest case — because a position that can't survive its best objection isn't worth holding.

#### Who are these three men?

Start with the youngest of the three, and the one whose story explains the most.

Athanasius Schneider was born in 1961 in Soviet Kyrgyzstan, to a family of German Catholics who had been deported east under Stalin. **[VERIFY: birthplace and year]** There were almost no open churches. The sacraments came in secret — a priest arriving at night, a Mass offered in a family's home behind closed curtains, the faithful kneeling on a wooden floor, everyone aware that being discovered could mean prison for the priest. In that world, nobody needed a definition of what the Church was. It was the Mass, the priest, the bishop he answered to, and the Pope far away in Rome. People risked everything for it precisely because it was clear.

In 1973 the family was allowed to leave for West Germany. Schneider later became a priest, then a bishop, and since 2011 has served as auxiliary bishop of Astana, in Kazakhstan — back in the same lands where his family's faith had survived underground. **[VERIFY: 2011 appointment]** When a man formed by that experience says the Church is being made vague, he is describing the loss of the one thing that kept his family Catholic.

Raymond Burke comes from the opposite end of the Church's life — not its catacombs, but its courts. He is a canon lawyer, former Archbishop of St. Louis, and from 2008 to 2014 served as Prefect of the Apostolic Signatura, the Church's supreme tribunal. Benedict XVI made him a cardinal in 2010. For a canonist, words are not decoration. A term in Church law has to mean something specific, because people's marriages, ordinations, and rights depend on it. When Burke says a term has no standing, he's speaking from inside that discipline.

And Gerhard Müller is a dogmatic theologian — a former professor, former Bishop of Regensburg, and editor of the collected works of Joseph Ratzinger. In 2012, Pope Benedict XVI appointed him Prefect of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, the office charged with guarding Catholic teaching. Pope Francis made him a cardinal two years later.

Law. Doctrine. And a faith that survived persecution. When all three raise the same concern, it deserves a hearing.

Their criticisms meet at three points. First, that *synodality* has no roots in the Council's texts. Second, that it has never been defined. Third, that what's being built under its name doesn't fit what the Council taught about who teaches in the Church.

Let's test each one. But first, the word itself.

#### Where the word comes from

*Synod* is Greek. *Syn* means "together." *Hodos* means "way" or "road." A synod is a coming-together on the road — which is where the popular phrase "walking together" comes from.

But in the Church's actual history, the word had a very specific meaning. From the early centuries, *synod* in Greek and *council* in Latin were used almost interchangeably. Both meant the same thing: bishops gathered to settle questions of faith and discipline. The great councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon were called synods. In the Eastern Churches today, the governing body is called the Holy Synod — and it is made of bishops.

So for most of Christian history, the word had a clear answer to the question, "who walks together, and to do what?" Bishops, gathered with Peter, to decide. Keep that in mind. It matters later.

#### Point one: is the word really absent?

We don't need a traditionalist source for this. In 2018, the International Theological Commission — the theologians who advise the Vatican's doctrinal office — published *Synodality in the Life and Mission of the Church*. In its opening section, the Commission acknowledges that the term and the concept of synodality do not explicitly appear in the teaching of Vatican II. **[VERIFY: ITC 2018, §5–6]**

It isn't in the 1983 Code of Canon Law either. The Code contains a whole section on the Synod of Bishops — canons 342 to 348. The institution is there. The abstract noun isn't.

So point one isn't an accusation. It's a fact the Vatican's own Commission concedes in writing.

In fairness, the same Commission goes on to argue that synodality, though unnamed, sits at the heart of the renewal the Council set in motion. That's a real argument and you should hear it. But notice what it asks you to accept: that the central idea of the Council is one its two thousand bishops never once put into words — and that its name arrived decades later, already carrying the Council's authority.

Cardinal Burke, in his foreword to *The Synodal Process Is a Pandora's Box*, describes synodality as a term with no history in the Church's doctrine and no reasonable definition. **[VERIFY: foreword wording]**

Point one: confirmed — by the Vatican's own theologians.

Now, to be completely accurate, there's something the Council *did* say, and you should know it, because someone will raise it in the comments.

The Council does talk about synods — the plural noun, the actual gatherings. In the decree on bishops, *Christus Dominus*, paragraph 36, it looks back with real admiration on the early centuries, when bishops joined together in synods and provincial councils to share their wisdom, settle questions of discipline, and protect the faith. And it expresses the wish that these venerable institutions of synods and councils flourish again. **[VERIFY: CD 36 wording]**

So the Council wasn't hostile to synods. It praised them.

But read who was in them. Bishops. Every synod the Council mentions is a gathering of bishops — the same meaning the word had held since Nicaea. The Council looked at the Church's synodal tradition and described it precisely: shepherds meeting with shepherds, in communion with Peter.

That's what makes the absence of *synodality* so striking. The Council had the word *synod* in its hands. It knew the history. It praised the institution. And it never once turned it into an abstract principle describing the whole Church — never said that the Church *is* synodal, in the way the Creed says she is one, holy, catholic, and apostolic.

That step came later. And when it came, the meaning of the word started to widen beyond what the Council had described.

#### How the Council handled its own new words

And here's a detail that makes the absence even more telling. The Council was not careless with new language. When it did introduce a major new emphasis, it went out of its way to define it.

The clearest example is *collegiality* — the teaching that the bishops form a college with the Pope at its head. It was one of the most fiercely debated subjects of the entire Council. Some fathers feared it would be read as weakening the authority of the Pope. So in November 1964, before *Lumen Gentium* was approved, a *Preliminary Note of Explanation* was attached to the text, spelling out exactly what collegiality meant and what it did not mean — including that the college never acts without its head, and that the Pope remains free to exercise his authority on his own.

Sit with that for a moment. When the Council fathers introduced a new concept that touched the structure of the Church, they refused to leave it vague. They defined it, fenced it, and attached the definition to the document itself.

Synodality received no such treatment — because it was never there to be defined.

#### Point two: has it ever been defined since?

Ask ten people what synodality means and you'll hear "walking together," "listening," "co-responsibility," "communion, participation, and mission." Each phrase is pleasant. None is a definition. None tells you what's permitted, what's required, or who decides.

Bishop Schneider has gone further, describing the term as a code word — left vague on purpose, so each faction can fill it with whatever it wants.

We can't prove anyone's intent, and I won't pretend to. But we can describe the effect. A defined term can be tested: held up against Scripture, Tradition, and the Council, and shown to fit or not fit. An undefined term can't be tested. It can only be invoked. And a word that can only be invoked is a convenient tool for anyone who wants change without having to defend it.

That's an argument, not a document. Weigh it yourself.

#### Point three: who teaches?

This is the heart of it, so let's read the Council directly.

*Lumen Gentium*, paragraph 22: the college of bishops holds authority only together with its head, the Roman Pontiff, and never without him. That's collegiality — genuine Council teaching. And the college is made of bishops.

*Lumen Gentium*, paragraph 12, the text most often cited for the synodal process: the whole body of the faithful, anointed by the Holy One, cannot err in matters of belief. That's true, and Catholics should hold it firmly. But keep reading. The Council says this sense of the faith is exercised under the guidance of the sacred teaching authority. The faithful receive the faith, hold fast to it, and enter into it more deeply.

And *Dei Verbum*, paragraph 10, says who interprets it: the task of authentically interpreting the Word of God has been entrusted exclusively to the living teaching office of the Church.

Read that again. *Exclusively*.

Now hear Pope Francis on October 17th, 2015, at the fiftieth anniversary of the Synod of Bishops. He described the synodal Church as an inverted pyramid, where the top is located beneath the base.

Read charitably, that's a reminder that bishops are servants — and they are. But a pyramid is a structure, and turning one over is a structural image. The Council's structure is plain: the faithful hold the faith; the bishops, with Peter, teach it with authority; the two roles are not interchangeable.

And this isn't only Vatican II. The First Vatican Council, in 1870 — the same council that defined papal infallibility — set a limit on it in the very same document. In *Pastor Aeternus*, chapter four, it teaches that the Holy Spirit was promised to the successors of Peter not so that they might make known some new doctrine by his revelation, but so that, with his help, they might guard the revelation handed down by the apostles and faithfully explain it.

Read that slowly. Even the Pope — the one man in the Church whose teaching can be infallible — is not given the Holy Spirit to invent new doctrine. His job is to guard what was handed down.

So if the successor of Peter himself has no authority to add to the deposit of faith, then no assembly does either — however large, however prayerful, however carefully it listens. Whatever synodality means, it can't mean that. And the reason that needs saying out loud is that an undefined word never says it for you.

#### The Council of Jerusalem

Defenders of synodality often reach further back than Vatican II — all the way to the Acts of the Apostles, chapter 15. It's the first council in the Church's history, and it's worth reading closely, because it's the model both sides claim.

The question was whether Gentile converts had to be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses. Paul and Barnabas went up to Jerusalem. Scripture says the apostles and the elders gathered to consider the matter. There was much debate. Peter stood and spoke. James gave his judgment. And then — verse 22 — it seemed good to the apostles and the elders, *with the whole church*, to choose men to carry the decision to Antioch.

So the whole church was involved. That's true, and it's in the text.

But look at the letter itself, in verse 23. It begins: "The apostles and the elders, brothers, to the brethren who are of the Gentiles." And the decision is announced with the most remarkable sentence in the chapter: "It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us." **[VERIFY: translation used on screen]**

The whole community took part in receiving and carrying the decision. The apostles and elders made it. That's the oldest model of a synod the Church has — and it's the same structure the Council of 1965 would describe.

Here's what the Council settles, and what it leaves open. It settles who teaches. It doesn't say a word about synodality — because the word wasn't there. So the question becomes: does what's been built since stay inside the lines the Council drew? For that, we need the record.

#### The record

**1965:** Paul VI creates the Synod of Bishops. Canon 343 still describes its role today — to discuss the questions put before it and make recommendations, not to resolve them or issue decrees, unless the Pope grants it deliberative power in a given case. A consultative body of bishops, serving Peter.

**2015:** the inverted pyramid speech.

**2018:** Pope Francis issues *Episcopalis Communio*, which restructures the synod into a multi-year process beginning with consultation of the faithful, and provides that a synod's final document, once expressly approved by the Pope, participates in his ordinary Magisterium. **[VERIFY: article 18]**

**2021:** the Synod on Synodality opens worldwide.

**2023:** for the first time, laypeople — men and women — vote as members of the assembly that still bears the name Synod of *Bishops*. **[VERIFY: number of non-bishop voting members]**

#### The objection you'll hear

"The Council created the synod. Synodality comes straight from Vatican II."

Half of that is true. Paul VI announced the synod while the Council was still in session, and the Council's decree on bishops, *Christus Dominus*, welcomed it.

But look at what that synod was: bishops, gathered to advise the Pope. That's the institution the Council era produced. And step by step, the record shows that institution being reshaped — its process, its membership, the weight of its documents. You can't appeal to an institution as your foundation while rebuilding it into something else. That's the tension all three men have named.

The strongest answer the other side has — one that comes from a saint — and the reply to it. Each man's specific warning. And a September morning in 1965 when Paul VI told the bishops exactly what the synod was for.

### PART 2

Arguments about words can feel abstract. So before we hear the objections, let's look at what happened when one national Church took synodality and ran with it — because it's the clearest test case we have.

#### The German Synodal Way

In 2019, the bishops of Germany, together with the country's leading lay Catholic organization, launched what they called the *Synodaler Weg* — the Synodal Way. Its assemblies were made up of bishops and laypeople voting together. Over the next four years, those assemblies approved texts calling for blessings of same-sex couples, for re-examining the Church's teaching on sexuality, for studying the ordination of women, and for a permanent synodal council of bishops and laity to govern the Church in Germany going forward. **[VERIFY: resolutions as summarised]**

That's what synodality looked like when a group of bishops was free to define it for themselves.

And Rome noticed. In June 2019, before the process even began, Pope Francis wrote a long letter to the Catholics of Germany, warning them not to treat the Church as something to be restructured by human effort, and urging them to stay in communion with the universal Church.

In July 2022, the Holy See issued a public statement: the Synodal Way, it said, has no power to oblige bishops and the faithful to adopt new forms of governance or new approaches to doctrine and morals. **[VERIFY: Holy See statement, 21 July 2022]**

And Pope Francis himself, speaking about Germany in 2022, put it in one memorable line: there is already a very good Protestant church in Germany — we don't need two. **[VERIFY: source and wording]**

Notice what that means. When Bishop Schneider warns that synodality, left undefined, drifts toward Protestant-style governance, he is not saying anything harsher than what the Pope himself said about the most ambitious synodal project in the Church. The difference is that Schneider draws the lesson for the word, not just for one country. If a principle can be used to justify everything the German assemblies voted for — and Rome then has to step in and say "not that" — the principle was never clear enough to begin with.

Now, let's give the other side its due.

#### First objection: "The Synod is not a parliament."

Pope Francis said this repeatedly. The official texts affirm that bishops remain the Church's pastors and teachers, and that consultation is not a vote on doctrine. Defenders will say the critics are attacking something no one in Rome has proposed — and they'll point to Germany as proof that Rome itself drew the line.

It's a fair point, and here's the reply: if synodality is not a parliament, then tell us what it is. A negative definition — "it isn't this" — is not a definition. It rules out one thing and leaves everything else open. Germany shows exactly what happens in that open space. Precision is the only protection, and it's the one thing that's been missing.

#### Second objection — and this one comes from a saint

In 1859, John Henry Newman wrote an essay called *On Consulting the Faithful in Matters of Doctrine*. In it, he argued that during the Arian crisis of the fourth century, when many bishops wavered on the divinity of Christ, it was in large part the ordinary faithful who held fast to the faith of Nicaea. Saint Jerome described that era by saying the whole world groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian.

So, the objection goes: the laity have saved the Church before. Shouldn't they be heard now?

That's the strongest card the other side holds. But look at what those faithful were actually doing. They weren't proposing anything new. They were clinging to a faith already defined — the Creed of Nicaea, built on a precise word, *homoousios*, "of one substance with the Father." Their witness was fidelity to a fixed definition, against bishops who wanted it softened.

Newman's own example points the other way. The faithful of the fourth century were heroes precisely because they refused ambiguity. And it's worth noticing that the bishop most associated with that resistance — Athanasius of Alexandria, exiled five times for refusing to give up a single word of the Creed — shares his name with one of the three men in this story.

#### Third objection: "Words develop."

*Homoousios* isn't in the Gospels. *Transubstantiation* isn't in Scripture. Why hold synodality to a different standard?

Because those words were adopted to make the faith more precise. They were argued over, defined, fenced against error, and fixed by councils. They closed questions. Synodality has gone the other way: it arrived after the Council, has never been defined by one, and is used to reopen what was settled. A new word that adds precision is development. A new word that removes it is something else.

And this standard isn't ours. It's ancient. In the year 434, Saint Vincent of Lérins wrote a short book called the *Commonitorium*, asking how a Christian can tell true teaching from error. His answer became famous: hold to what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all.

But Vincent didn't deny that doctrine develops. He compared it to a child growing into a man — the same person, larger, stronger, but never turning into something else. Growth, he said, must happen within its own kind: in the same doctrine, the same sense, the same meaning. The First Vatican Council quoted those exact words in 1870 and made them part of its teaching. **[VERIFY: Dei Filius ch. 4 citation]**

The same sense. The same meaning. If a new word can be used to mean the opposite of what the Church taught before, it fails Vincent's test before you even get to the details.

Newman himself, the same Newman the other side quotes, wrote a whole book on this in 1845: *An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine*. He gave seven marks of a true development. Two of them settle our question. The first he called preservation of type — a true development keeps the same essential shape, the way an oak keeps the nature of the acorn. Another he called conservative action upon its past — a true development protects and confirms what came before it; it doesn't undo it.

Hold synodality up to those two marks. Does it preserve the type of a synod — bishops gathered with Peter? Its membership has already changed. Does it confirm what came before? In Germany, it was used to challenge teaching the Church had held for centuries, until Rome stepped in. By Newman's own criteria, those are warning signs, not marks of growth.

#### Fourth objection: "The Orthodox are synodal — this is about unity."

Some defenders say synodality is an ecumenical bridge: the Orthodox Churches govern themselves through synods, so a more synodal Catholic Church is a step toward reunion.

There's real truth in the respect owed to the East. But look at what an Orthodox synod actually is. It's a synod of bishops. Laypeople do not vote on doctrine in an Orthodox Holy Synod. If the goal is to draw closer to the East, a process that gives laypeople votes in a synod of bishops moves in the opposite direction from the very Churches it claims to imitate.

#### What we are not saying

Before the three warnings, let's be clear about what this video is not claiming, because it's easy to hear criticism as something it isn't.

We are not saying that bishops shouldn't listen. Good shepherds always have. The Council itself, in its decree on the laity, *Apostolicam Actuositatem*, teaches that the lay faithful have the right, and sometimes the duty, to make their views known on matters concerning the good of the Church. That's Council teaching, and we accept it.

We are not saying the laity are second-class Catholics. *Lumen Gentium* gives them an entire chapter, and teaches that every baptized person shares in Christ's priestly, prophetic, and kingly office.

And we are not accusing any Pope of heresy. Schneider, Burke, and Müller haven't either. Their objection is to a word and a process — to vagueness — not to the papacy, which all three have defended their whole lives.

What we are saying is narrower, and harder to dismiss: listening is not the same as teaching. Consultation is not the same as authority. And a word that blurs those lines, without ever being defined, is a word the Church should not build her future on.

#### The three warnings — each tied to the record

**Cardinal Müller's concern is authority.** In 2022, he called the synodal process a hostile takeover of the Church of Jesus Christ. **[VERIFY: EWTN, October 2022]** Strip away the heat and his reasoning is classical theology: a bishop's authority to teach comes from Christ through ordination, not by delegation from the community. And the record shows the weight of the synod's documents rising — from recommendations in 1965 to potential participation in the ordinary Magisterium after 2018. When a consultative process can produce magisterial text, the question of where authority comes from is no longer abstract.

**Cardinal Burke's concern is definition.** It's a lawyer's rule: you don't sign a contract with a blank where the terms should be. His image of a Pandora's box isn't a claim that everything inside the process is false. It's a warning that once you accept an undefined governing principle, you've agreed in advance to whatever is later placed inside it.

And Burke didn't just write about it. In 2023, shortly before the synod's first assembly in Rome, he was one of five cardinals who formally submitted questions to Pope Francis — what the Church calls *dubia*. One of them asked directly about synodality: whether it could be presented as a constitutive dimension of the Church, in a way that would place the authority of the Pope and the bishops under the decisions of a synodal body. **[VERIFY: wording of the synodality dubium, 2023]** The question was, at its root, a request for a definition. Which is exactly what this entire debate is missing.

**Bishop Schneider's concern is direction.** He has warned that the vagueness serves a movement toward consensus-based governance like that of many Protestant communities, where doctrine can be revised by assembly. That sounds harsh, so let's be exact. It's a structural comparison, not an insult. And the direction he describes — from bishops alone, to voting laypeople, in a body that kept its old name — is on the record. Germany showed where it can lead when nobody stops it. You can dispute where the universal Church is heading. You can't dispute that it moved.

#### Back to 1965

On the morning of September 14th, 1965, Paul VI opened the fourth and final session of the Council in Saint Peter's Basilica. Before the assembled bishops, he announced a new body: a Synod of Bishops, drawn from around the world, to assist the Pope in his care for the universal Church.

The next day he made it law, in the motu proprio *Apostolica Sollicitudo*. Read it, and you'll find its purposes laid out plainly. The synod was to foster close union between the Pope and the bishops of the world. It was to give the Pope direct, accurate information about the state of the Church in different places. And it was to help the bishops reach agreement on essential points of doctrine and on how the Church should act. By its nature, the document says, its task is to inform and to advise — and it can decide only when the Pope grants it that power. **[VERIFY: summary of Apostolica Sollicitudo, sections I–II]**

Union with Peter. Information. Advice. Agreement on doctrine — not revision of it.

He was speaking to bishops, about bishops. Its purpose was clear, its membership defined, its authority modest. Everyone in that basilica knew what it was.

Sixty years later, we have a word built on that institution's name — absent from the Council, absent from the Code, admitted to be new by the Vatican's own theologians, and never authoritatively defined.

#### What does an ordinary Catholic do with this?

Not panic, and not leave. Schneider, Burke, and Müller have raised every one of these concerns from inside the Church, as her bishops. That's the model.

And when a new term is presented to you as the teaching of the Church, ask three questions.

**One:** where is it in the documents? Search the text. *Lumen Gentium* and *Dei Verbum* are short, and free online.

**Two:** what does it mean, exactly? If no one can tell you what it requires and what it forbids, you've been handed a slogan, not a teaching.

**Three:** does it make the faith clearer, or blurrier? Every true development in the Church's history has added precision. Be wary of anything that takes it away.

Let's try it on the phrase you'll hear most: "walking together."

Question one: is it in the documents? The image of the Church as a pilgrim people on a journey is real — *Lumen Gentium* speaks of the pilgrim Church. So far, so good.

Question two: what does it mean? Walking together — toward where? Led by whom? Who decides the route when people disagree? The phrase doesn't say. And on a real road, those are the only questions that matter.

Question three: does it make the faith clearer? Here's the test. Two people can say "we're walking together" while one heads toward the Creed and the other heads away from it. The phrase covers both. It doesn't help you tell them apart. That's not a teaching. That's a mood.

Now compare it with what the Church actually gave us. The Creed doesn't say "we walk together." It says: *I believe in one, holy, catholic, and apostolic Church.* One. Holy. Catholic. Apostolic. Four words, each one defined, each one tested over centuries, each one telling you exactly where the road goes and who leads on it.

That's why this channel stands with these three men. Not because they're comfortable figures, and not because they tell us what we'd like to hear. Because while others argued over what synodality should mean, they asked the earlier question: where did this word come from, and why did the Council never use it?

Think back to that house in Soviet Central Asia, curtains drawn, a priest arriving in the dark. Nobody in that room needed a new word to describe the Church. They knew exactly what it was — clearly enough to risk prison for it.

Sixteen documents. More than six hundred articles. Not one mention.

The documents are still there. Read them. Pray for the Pope and for the bishops. And hold fast — the way the faithful of the fourth century did, and the way that family in Kyrgyzstan did — to a faith that is precise because it was given to us by Christ, who built His Church on Peter and the apostles.

## 4. Visual System

### What the reference channel does

| Element | Observation |

| **Footage** | Full-frame stock B-roll: church interiors, drone shots of St. Peter's, crowds in the Square, altars, stained glass. Warm golden grade on interiors, sunset grade on Rome aerials |

| **Graphics** | None — no documents, quote cards, name tags, or maps |
| **Pacing** | New clip every ~4–8 seconds; slow push-ins, drone moves, static wides |

### Weaknesses we fix

1. **Generic B-roll that doesn't match the narration** (e.g. "angry parish priest venting online" over a reverent Latin Mass).
2. **No evidence on screen** — the channel says "check the documents" but never shows one.
3. **Caption errors on proper nouns** ("RORATE KELLY" for *Rorate Caeli*).
4. **AI imagery presented like real footage.**
5. **"BREAKING NEWS" on everything**, including evergreen content.

### Our rules

- **Captions:** same style as reference, but proofread every proper noun against the spelling list below.
- **Document cards (new signature element):** cream parchment card, document title + article number, the quoted line highlighted in gold. Used for every sourced claim.
- **Name tags:** licensed or press photos of real people with a lower-third tag. **Never AI-generate a real person's face.**
- **Timeline graphic:** horizontal 1965 → 2015 → 2018 → 2021 → 2023 with a moving gold marker.
- **B-roll rule:** every clip must illustrate the line it plays under. If nothing fits, use a document card.
- **Disclosure:** apply YouTube's "altered or synthetic content" label to any AI-generated scenery.
